# Scene A — v2: Bodily / Sensory Texture

The tide table said twenty minutes to three. At thirty-three minutes past two, black stone showed between the mainland and Saltreach, first in separate crowns, then in a joined road scored by two wheel tracks. Ada set her case on the dry grit above the tide line and kept her glove around its handle. Water slid east across the stones. Weed lifted, flattened, lifted again. Her watch ticked against the pulse in her wrist, seven minutes ahead of the printed figure, and she did not step down.

The table in the glass-fronted box had swollen at its lower corners. Its columns were narrow enough to rule with a thumbnail, and the afternoon ebb stood between morning and evening in clean black type. Beside the box, salt had opened grey scales in the warning board. The causeway keeper's hut was gone. On the island marker, a white iron ring shone through brush marks, with water touching its underside. Ada rubbed her thumb along the case handle until the leather warmed. Beyond the ring, two rows of slate roofs caught the afternoon; the western rise hid the lighthouse.

At forty-one minutes past, a pony cart rolled down between the houses and stopped at the far marker. The driver stood and whistled with two fingers. Wind tore the answering shout flat across the water, but the scarf over the driver's hair held its red. Ada lifted her hand. The cart came on, iron rims knocking at the joints, the pony's fetlocks dark to the bone. When it drew near, the woman in the man's oilskin had Mara Quill's scarf, Mara's square hands, and a pale seam across one knuckle that the blunt knife on the old net-shed block had left when two girls cut stolen wool in half. Mara had wrapped the knuckle in one red piece and tied the other around Ada's wrist. Now the scarf ends struck her oilskin collar while the wheel pushed a ridge of water ahead of it.

The left wheel stopped in a runnel. Mara looked at Ada, then down at the case. "That's a small case for twenty-one years." Ada's fingers closed harder on the handle. "Four shirts, two skirts, and my work clothes." Mara nudged the postbag away with her boot. "Bookkeepers need a uniform now?" "Only for dangerous sums." Mara's laugh made the pony toss its head. She climbed down, put both arms around Ada, and drew her against cold oilcloth. Ada's gloves were caught between their ribs. The case buckle knocked her shin. When Mara let go, she took the case without asking and heaved it into the cart. "Get in before the road goes greasy under you."

Tarred rope lay in a black coil beneath the seat. Its fibres had stiffened into hooks, and the damp brought the tar smell through the wool of Ada's coat. Three flour sacks filled the crate behind them; a bruised apple rolled at every dip and struck the same board. Mara turned only when the white ring stood clear by the width of her finger. The pony stepped onto the wet stone, shoes striking, slipping half an inch, striking again. Ada put her case beside the rope and braced one boot against it. Nine marker posts stood where six had been. She counted each black band and then the spaces between, touching thumb to fingertip under her folded gloves.

At the fourth marker, Ada laid one glove over the other. "He said it was rheumatism." Mara shortened the right rein. "It may be." "You wrote that he dropped the lamp chimney." Mara drew the pony around a stone with one corner broken away. "He dropped two. I only told you about one." The wheel entered a hollow and brown water slapped the floorboard. Ada lifted her heels until it drained. "Why?" Mara ran the rein through her palm, took up the slack, and pointed the pony toward the crown of the road. "After the first, you sent back his own words. I wasn't posting them a second time."

Ada folded the gloves along their old seams. Her thumbnail caught the worn piping at the forefinger and pulled it crooked. She flattened it, opened both gloves, and began again. Mara kept the pony between the wheel tracks until the sixth marker passed behind them; there she set the reins across one thigh and retied the scarf, tugging the knot once beneath her ear. The pale seam on her knuckle disappeared into the red wool. "I wrote every month for the first year after you went," she said. "You answered twice. Tom died, then Lena, then you went, and you left no word for me at all. Now I send four lines about Joss and you cross in a week. Don't climb into my cart and call that last Tuesday. You left without saying goodbye to me." Ada stopped smoothing the gloves. She laid them on the flour sacks, reached across the postbag, and pulled the loose end of Mara's scarf free where the knot had trapped it. She folded the end back once and tucked it flat beneath the knot, covering the old white seam in Mara's hand. Then she put her case lengthwise between their boots instead of at the edge of the cart. Mara picked up the reins. "That will do." At the seventh marker she pointed her chin toward three muddy fields and said the turnips had formed a government. Ada's mouth opened before the laugh came; Mara's came at once, broad enough to send the pony's ears back, and the two sounds travelled with the cart toward the houses.
