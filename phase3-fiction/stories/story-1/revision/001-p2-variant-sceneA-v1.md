# Scene A — v1: Inner Development

The tide table said the causeway would be passable at twenty minutes to three, and at thirty-three minutes past two Ada Vane could see enough of the middle stones to understand how a person less accustomed to figures might mistake appearance for permission. The black crowns rose one after another between mainland and Saltreach; grit appeared, then weed-dark joints, then the paired wheel tracks in which the last water ran east. Her watch agreed with the printed column. Neither took account of the wind pushing against the ebb, or of the thin skin that made a fixed stone seem to slide beneath it, and she had spent twenty-one years finding the small fact omitted from an otherwise correct account. She kept one gloved hand on her case and waited. When she was eight, her father had let her call the crossing safe as soon as the iron ring on the island marker showed red, and she had announced it with such authority that he stopped the cart, although he had already seen the ring himself; for the length of the causeway she believed she had held the sea off by naming what she saw. The ring was white now, the causeway keeper's hut gone, her father's cart replaced by whatever vehicle someone had been sent to meet her in, but the old error remained tempting: that a sign observed correctly must also obey. Seven minutes early was not early enough to trust.

She had come back to see how much her father had left out of his letters. She meant to reach the lighthouse before dark, look at his hands, and decide how long she would stay, but the third purpose had occupied the train journey without becoming clearer for all the hours she gave it. A week would insult the bluntness of Mara's note. A month, which Ada had written to Joss, could be entered at the stationer's as an absence with a beginning and an end. Anything longer opened a blank column into which her room, the quarterly rents, the clothes she had not packed, and the life that had acquired its shape by being repeated might slide without a total. She was afraid of that blankness in a way she had not been afraid of the letter saying two lamp chimneys had broken in one season, and because the fear supplied no useful instruction she turned her attention to the marker. Water still touched the lower edge of its badly painted ring. Beyond it, Saltreach showed as two uneven rows of roofs on the sheltered shore, close enough for the windows to catch the afternoon and too far for her to tell whether anyone stood behind them. The lighthouse remained hidden on the western point, letting her think of a building for another minute instead of the man whose hand had formed every brief, steady address she had received since leaving.

At forty-one minutes past, a pony cart came down between the houses and stopped at the island marker. Its driver stood, put two fingers to her mouth, and whistled across half a mile of wet stone. Ada raised one hand. The answer the woman shouted went sideways in the wind, but the red scarf tied over her hair did not, and Ada knew Mara Quill first by the colour. Mara had worn one like it when they were girls, stolen from her father's drawer and divided on the net-shed block with a knife too blunt for wool. They had pulled the scarf between them and sawed until the weave gave, then disputed which half was longer, laying one over the other, reversing them, smoothing the fringe, unable to make the diagonal tear answer to a ruler. Ada's half still lay in a biscuit tin beneath old receipts on the mainland. She had kept it because throwing it away would have required admitting it was kept, then forgotten it so completely that its return now seemed less like memory than an unpaid item brought forward from a closed book. As the cart crossed toward her, she remembered the wool biting into her palms, loose red fibres on her cuff, Mara swearing at the knife, and finally Mara's younger face—not as it had looked, but bent close beside hers over two unequal pieces while both pretended equality could be made by attention alone.

The cart stopped with one wheel in a runnel. Mara was broader than the girl Ada had stored away, wrapped in a man's oilskin, with a postbag against one boot and the scarf knotted badly beneath her ear. She looked from Ada to the case. "That's a small case for twenty-one years," she said. Ada held the handle. "Four shirts, two skirts, and my work clothes." Mara shifted the postbag aside. "Have they made bookkeepers wear special clothes now?" Ada said, "Only when the sums are dangerous," and Mara's laugh startled the pony into raising its head. For a moment the years did not vanish; they crowded together, all twenty-one of them, between the sound Ada remembered and the altered body making it. Mara climbed down and caught her around the shoulders. The oilskin was cold. Ada's hands, still holding her gloves, remained trapped between them until the buckle of her case pressed her shin and Mara released her. "Put that in. Unless the mainland taught you to walk on weed without breaking your neck."

The cart smelled of tarred rope, damp wool, and apples. A blackened coil lay beneath the seat, the ferry's spare painter, Mara explained, used to tie the boat at the landing; Ada placed her case beside it and the three flour sacks. Mara waited for the white ring to clear by a finger's breadth before turning the pony onto the stones, and that was the first thing Ada recognized without having to search backward for it. Brown weed lay across the farther stones. Water hurried through the gaps, and the pony chose its feet while Ada counted the nine marker posts twice, first as posts and then as intervals. At the fourth she folded her gloves together. "He said it was rheumatism." Mara watched the road. "It may be." Ada followed the right rein as it moved between Mara's fingers. "You wrote that he dropped the lamp chimney." Mara drew the pony around a broken stone. "He dropped two. I only told you about one." The cart settled after the hollow before Ada asked why. "Because after the first you wrote back that he said it was nothing, and I knew what sort of arithmetic we were doing."

Ada pressed each glove finger flat along its old crease and put her father's letters into their monthly order in her mind: wind, wall, roof, damp knuckles; repair following complaint as proof that complaint had no consequence. Her own replies had contained rents, mainland weather, and money orders returned untouched. Each letter was exact. None had required a second page. She had taken that economy for resemblance between them when it had also been concealment, and was ashamed now of how readily a shared habit had excused them both. "You could have written plainly," she said, catching a nail in the cloth and spoiling the crease. Mara guided the cart around another flooded hollow. "I did. You're here." At the sixth marker she loosened the reins but did not look across. "I was glad when you answered. I was angry it took my letter to bring you, and I was glad all the same. Both can sit in the cart; there's room. I don't expect you to say you missed me, because you never did like owing anybody a sentence. But don't come back and make me pretend you only went away last Tuesday. You left, Ada. I minded it." Ada unfolded the gloves and began again with the little finger, understanding that no arrangement would make the words weigh less and that an answer could not be made accurate by making it small. "I know." Mara nodded toward the road. "Good. That's the real thing said. We can discuss turnips now." Ada asked how many there were. "A damned multitude. Saltreach is lost under them." Mara grinned, and Ada laughed with her as the pony carried them beyond the seventh marker.
