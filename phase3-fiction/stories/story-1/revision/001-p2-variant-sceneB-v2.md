# Scene B — v2: Bodily / Sensory Texture

The tower stairs wound around a cold iron post. Joss kept his right hand on the rail and his left against his coat. At the first landing, four full oil cans stood behind one open can, their weights chalked on the black sides. At the second, curved wick scissors hung in a leather loop above a shelf of folded cloths. Joss's fingers opened beside the loop, brushed the wall, and went on. Ada lifted the scissors free. Their bent points were stained brown behind the hinge. She carried them upward with the logbook pressed under her other arm.

Glass ringed the lantern room. The lamp stood in the centre, brass base warm from the afternoon sun, prisms throwing white bars over the worktable. Joss set down the hot-water can. He picked up a cloth, pushed it inside the chimney, and turned his wrist in tight circles. The rim clicked against his ring. On the third circle the glass struck the brass guard with a sharp note. Ada put the logbook beneath the south window and held out both hands. "Give me that." Joss raised the chimney to the light. A grey crescent clouded the upper edge. "It's nearly clean." Ada kept her hands out. He set it in them.

She wrapped the cloth around two fingers and wiped the crescent away. Joss reached toward the leather loop, found it empty, and looked at the scissors lying by Ada's wrist. "How long?" she asked. He opened a cupboard, checked the oil strainer, and shut it. Ada laid one hand flat beside the scissors. "Since your hands began to shake." Joss put his fingers through the handles. The points opened over the blackened wick, met above it, and opened again. His thumbnail went white against the loop. "Since March. Worse since August. The right is better in the morning. The left is no use for fine work. I can fill the reservoir and wind the lens weight, but I can't always get the char even. An uneven wick smokes, the chimney clouds, and the light loses distance. There. That's the account."

Ada turned the cleaning cloth until its grey fold lay inside. "You should have told me in March." Joss rested the heel of his hand on the burner. "You had work." "I have work now." The scissor points travelled a shallow arc over the wick and did not touch it. Ada opened her hand. "Show me the proper angle." Joss held the scissors above her palm for the length of three ticks from the tower clock, then released them. The lower loop landed first. Ada slid her fingers through.

Joss put one finger beside the wick without touching the char. "Not straight across. Leave a little crown in the middle. Take only the crust. Cut the sound cloth and it'll burn low until you wind it up." Ada closed the blades. A black thread remained at the left edge. She opened them, turned her wrist, and cut again; the thread bent under the blade. Joss rotated the burner with the heavy wheel, his palm flat against the rim. On Ada's third cut the char dropped in a curved strip, held its shape on the tray, and broke when his sleeve stirred the air. He pointed to the second wick. She moved the scissors there. He kept the burner turning under her hand.

When the last edge lay even, Ada set the scissors in the leather loop. She lowered the chimney and fastened its catch. Joss held the taper to the wick; blue travelled around the burner and thickened into yellow. Ada raised the control. A brown tongue marked the glass. Joss tapped once below it, and she turned the control back until the mark stopped growing. The lens began to move. Light crossed Joss's coat, the south pane, Ada's hands, then the sea; it returned and crossed them again.

Below, water covered the causeway. Nine marker tops stood in a diminishing row. At the ferry landing a green lamp came on, winked out behind the turning lens frame, and returned. Ada kept two fingers on the control. Joss put the cloth with the used ones and reached for the logbook. She moved it beneath her forearm.

"You can take the first watch," he said. "I'll lie down until ten." Ada bent to the oil gauge; the meniscus trembled against the etched mark and settled. "And after ten?" Joss took the empty water can by its wire handle. "We'll see." Ada stood with the logbook against her body. "No. We have seen. I'll take the night." His right hand tightened on the handle. The left closed over it but added no lift. His mouth opened, shut, and opened again. "I can't do it alone." Ada turned back to the burner. One edge had risen. She lowered it by the width of the line cut into the control and watched the flame pass twice through the lens without smoke. "Tonight isn't alone."

Joss set the can down. He placed her finger against the filling mark, opened the drawer of spare matches, and pressed the north pane until it rattled in its frame. His fingertip left a crescent on the glass. He wiped it with his sleeve and went down the stair. The rail gave one faint complaint at each turn. Ada counted twenty-seven steps; after the seventeenth there was a gap of two tower-clock ticks, then the tread sounded and the count continued until the door shut below.
