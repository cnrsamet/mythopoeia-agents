# Chapter One

The tide table said the causeway would be passable at twenty minutes to three. At thirty-three minutes past two, Ada Vane could already see the black crowns of the middle stones, one after another, between the mainland and Saltreach, but she stood beside her case and watched the water draw itself off the road, breaking first around the grit, then the weed-dark joints, then two shallow wheel tracks. Seven minutes early was not early enough to trust. The table had been printed in columns narrow enough to rule with a thumbnail, and the afternoon figure stood where it ought to stand between the morning ebb and evening flood; yet the sea had never agreed that a printed minute was an instruction, only a prediction made by people who would not have to put a boot on the stones. Ada had spent twenty-one years entering rents, adding invoices, and correcting sums copied badly from one book to another, so she knew the difference between a number being clear and a number being true. Her watch agreed with the table but accounted for neither wind nor the skin of water sliding eastward until the stone beneath seemed to move. She waited while the road assembled itself, crown by black crown, with one gloved hand on the case as though the island might otherwise claim that first.

She had come back to see how much her father had left out of his letters. She meant to reach the lighthouse before dark, look at his hands, and decide how long she would have to stay, the first two purposes plain and the third occupying most of the train journey without producing a figure. A week would insult whatever Mara had seen; a month, which she had written to Joss, could be entered and defended like any other absence from work; longer than that opened a blank column in which the stationer's rents, her own room, and the clothes she had not packed lost their proper lines. The causeway keeper had been gone for years, and where his hut had stood there was only a board now, grey with salt, warning strangers not to cross until the marker at the island end stood clear to its iron ring. Ada remembered a red ring because, as a child, she had watched for it from her father's cart and believed the colour held the water back; this one had been painted white, though badly, and water still touched its lower edge. Beyond it the town showed as two uneven rows of slate roofs on the sheltered shore, near enough for windows to catch the afternoon and too far for her to distinguish anyone looking back. The lighthouse did not show. It stood on the other side of the island, where the land turned its back on the town, letting her think for another minute of a building rather than the man inside it.

At forty-one minutes past, a pony cart came down between the houses and stopped at the marker. The driver stood, put two fingers to her mouth, and whistled across half a mile of wet stone; Ada lifted one hand, and the driver shouted something the wind took away before she slapped the reins against the pony's broad back and brought the cart onto the causeway. Mara Quill had still worn her hair in a long plait when Ada last saw her and was now a large woman in a man's oilskin, with a postbag wedged against one boot and a red scarf tied over her hair. Ada knew the scarf first. Mara had worn one like it as a girl, stolen from her father's drawer and cut in half on the net-shed block with a knife too blunt for cloth, so that they had sawed at the wool until its edges pulled crooked and each could have a piece; Ada's half remained in a mainland biscuit tin under old receipts, though she had not thought of it in years. The memory arrived not as Mara's younger face but as the pressure of their hands pulling the scarf taut, the rasp of the knife, and the importance of making the halves equal when no measurement could make a diagonal tear fair. By the time the cart reached her, Ada had remembered the loose red fibres on her cuff more clearly than she remembered saying goodbye.

The cart halted with its left wheel in a runnel of seawater, and Mara looked down at Ada, then at the case. "That's a small case for twenty-one years," she said. Ada kept hold of the handle while she answered, "Four shirts, two skirts, and my work clothes." Mara shifted the postbag with her heel and asked, "Have they made bookkeepers wear special clothes now?" "Only when the sums are dangerous." Mara gave a single loud laugh, startling the pony into lifting its head, and for a moment Ada could see the girl she had known, not in Mara's face but in the way the sound occupied more space than it needed and expected the world to make room. Mara climbed down and caught Ada around the shoulders; the oilskin was cold, and Ada's hands remained between them, still holding her gloves, until the buckle of the case pressed against her shin and Mara let go. "Put your case in," Mara said. "Unless you've learned to carry luggage over weed without breaking your neck."

The mail cart smelled of tarred rope, damp wool, and apples. The rope lay in a neat coil under the seat, blackened against rot; it was the ferry's spare painter, Mara said, used to tie the boat at the landing, and Ada set her case beside it and the crate holding three flour sacks. Mara looked once at the white ring, clear now by a finger's breadth, before she turned the pony, the first thing about her that Ada recognized without effort. For the first hundred yards the causeway stones were merely wet; farther on, brown weed lay over them in ropes, water hurried through the gaps on either side, and the pony chose its footing without guidance. Ada kept her eyes on the marker posts. There were nine now instead of the six she remembered, each with a black band showing the safe line, and she counted them twice, first as posts and then as intervals, because the second method sometimes found what the first had missed. When they passed the fourth, she folded her gloves together. "He said it was rheumatism," she said.

Mara looked ahead. "It may be." Ada watched the right rein move between Mara's fingers before saying, "You wrote that he had dropped the lamp chimney." "He dropped two. I only told you about one." The pony leaned into a shallow rise in the stones, and Ada waited until the cart settled before she asked, "Why?" Mara pulled the pony a little to the right of a broken stone. "Because after the first you wrote back that he said it was nothing, and I knew what sort of arithmetic we were doing." Ada folded her gloves along their old creases, pressing each finger flat with her thumb until the worn white edges met, while she set her father's letters in their monthly order: the first ferry bringing an envelope whose address never varied, the week's highest wind entered as if it were an expense, the state of the west wall noted as if she had requested an inspection, and, since spring, brief complaints about the damp in his knuckles, always placed after some repair that proved the hands had done their work. Her replies had contained the rent she collected for the stationer, the weather on the mainland, and money orders he returned untouched, a correspondence accurate in every particular that could be measured and therefore able to pass for complete; neither set of letters had required a second page, and until Mara's blunt note arrived Ada had treated that economy as one of the habits she and Joss shared rather than a space in which two broken chimneys could disappear.

"You could have written plainly," Ada said, although the complaint cost her one smooth crease in the gloves when her nail caught the cloth. Mara guided the cart around another flooded hollow and said, "I did. You're here." At the sixth marker she spoke again, louder, though the wind had dropped: "I was glad when you answered. I was angry it took my letter to bring you, and I was glad all the same. Both can sit in the cart; there's room. I don't expect you to say you missed me, because you never did like owing anybody a sentence. But don't come back and make me pretend you only went away last Tuesday. You left, Ada. I minded it." The white edges of Ada's gloves no longer met precisely, so she unfolded them and began again, taking time over the little finger while Mara's words remained in the cart with the postbag and the damp rope, things that would not become lighter because she found a better arrangement for them. "I know." Mara nodded toward the road and said, "Good. That's the real thing said. We can discuss turnips now." Ada refolded one glove over the other before asking, "Are there many?" "A damned multitude. Saltreach is lost under them." Mara grinned, and this time Ada laughed with her, the sound smaller but crossing the same strip of water while the pony carried them past the seventh marker.

By the ninth marker, the town had resolved into particular houses, each roof separating from the next and each chimney taking its own angle against the sky. Ada knew the post by the blue enamel sign beside its door, though the lower room had once been Tom Quill's net shed, and a ferry waited at the stone landing beyond it, painted green and white, broad enough for six passengers if four were friendly. Mara followed Ada's look. "She runs better than she looks," she said. "Unlike her owner." Ada examined the line of the ferry's gunwale, which rose a little at the bow but sat even against the landing, and asked, "Is she yours?" Mara loosened the reins as the pony found the dry road. "Mine and the bank's. Your father pays for the ferry's lamp oil, though I've told him the post can afford it. He says a boat ought to show where it is." Ada looked back at the westward road, but the lighthouse was still hidden by the rise, so all she could see of her father's care was a green-painted boat with a lamp not yet lit. "He likes a light accounted for." Mara's mouth changed, not quite closing, and her hand lifted as if she meant to rub at it; then she flicked the reins and called the pony a scholar for stepping over a rut.

They passed the forty houses slowly. Ada did not count them, because she knew there had been forty when she left and did not want to discover that memory had kept bad books; even refusing the count required her to resist assigning a number at every gate. A woman at the bakehouse door raised a floury hand, and two men mending pots outside the inn stopped their work; Ada had prepared herself for faces she could not name, but not for people who knew hers. At the road that climbed west, Mara stopped the cart. "Post first," she said. "Then I'll take you up." Ada reached for her case. "I can walk."

Mara rested one hand on the brake. "You can. You'll arrive cross and wet, and Joss will say that proves the mainland has made you foolish." Ada put both feet on the road but left one hand on the case. "What does my arriving in your cart prove?" Mara gathered the postbag into her lap. "That it has made you teachable. Leave the case. Come in and drink something while I sort this lot." The case stayed where it was, its smallness exposed among three sacks of flour, while Ada followed Mara beneath the enamel sign.

The post occupied the front room of Mara's house, and pigeonholes covered one wall, each labelled in the same blunt capitals—BELL, CROWE, FENN, QUILL, VANE—with no need for street names on an island with forty houses. Mara tipped the postbag onto the counter and put a kettle on the little iron stove; Ada stood opposite her and sorted without being asked: nine letters, four bills, a seed catalogue, two newspapers three days old, and a parcel that smelled strongly of soap. Her hands knew the work before the room. They straightened the envelopes, turned the stamps the same way, and separated demands from personal letters by the quality of paper, until the years between this counter and the mainland stationer's desk seemed no more important than the difference between two ledgers. Mara objected only when Ada placed the bills together. "If you give people all their bad news at once, they blame the postmistress. Mix in a letter from a nephew."

Ada held a bill for Crowe in one hand and a thin envelope in the other, weighing an order that would not change either message. "That alters the order, not the total." Mara took both from her and slid the letter on top. "It alters the walk home." The kettle took too long on the damp stove, so Mara found biscuits in three tins and set them all before Ada. One batch was burnt, one soft, and one tasted faintly of onions; they ate the onion ones after Mara tried one first and looked offended enough that Ada had to test the evidence.

Mara poured the tea dark and pushed the least chipped cup across the counter. "Your room at the light has been shut since Lena died. I opened the window last week. Joss shut it again." Ada put down her cup with the handle aligned to the saucer's faded flower; no one on the mainland said her mother's name unless Ada said it first, and she seldom did, so hearing it in the room where Lena Vane had once collected letters left Ada with nowhere prepared to look. "He knew I was coming." Mara rubbed a crumb from the counter into her palm and said, "Knowing isn't the same as letting air in. Your mother used to say that about him." Ada's fingers tightened on the cup handle, though the tea was too hot to lift. "What else did she say?" Mara looked at her then, fully and without the shelter of a joke. "That he'd mistake silence for strength until something broke under it. She loved him. She was tired of him. Those aren't enemies either."

In the pigeonhole marked VANE lay one envelope addressed in Ada's own hand, the note she had posted three days before leaving, though she had also sent a wire to the mainland inn. Joss had not come down for it. Mara saw where Ada was looking and said, "He hasn't fetched the post since Monday." Ada calculated the days without needing to look at the date stamp. "Today is Thursday." Mara dropped the last onion biscuit back into its tin. "I can count to three." Ada finished her tea, and the cup clicked against the saucer; she kept her fingers on it until it was still, then turned the envelope face down before helping Mara put the remaining letters into the pigeonholes. She could not improve the fact that it had waited there, but she could keep her own handwriting from looking at her while they worked.

The road to the western point rose between low stone walls. Mara drove, the town dropped behind them roof by roof, and the wind returned with nothing to stop it, bending the field grass steadily east. At the top of the rise the lighthouse appeared all at once: a white tower joined to a square keeper's house, set above a black descent of rock, less tall than the tower Ada had carried away in memory and more exposed, every wall showing what salt had done to its paint. Beyond it, the sea had no sheltered colour. Far below the headland, the Teeth were beginning to uncover as the tide withdrew, dark points in a ragged line, and Ada looked away before she had counted them, but their order remained behind her eyes: the first low enough for most waves to cover, the next shouldering through foam, the farther ones leading toward the water where the *Wren* had gone down. She did not permit herself the number. Numbers belonged to things that could be checked, entered, and carried forward, while this one had been told to her in lowered voices until it included the men on the ship, the people on shore, the years before her birth, and her father's silence after her mother's death, a total altered whenever someone refused to name one of its parts. She fixed on the keeper's house instead, counting two chimney pots, three western windows, the rain barrel, and one man waiting in a blue coat.

Joss Vane wore his keeper's coat despite the mild afternoon. At sixty-seven he was narrower than he had been in Ada's memory, though perhaps memory had added the width a child required. His beard was white around the mouth and clipped as evenly as ever, and he held one hand closed inside the other. Mara drew up beside the rain barrel. "Delivery for you. Slightly delayed."

Joss kept his eyes on the cart. "Tide was late," he said. Mara set the brake with her boot. "Tide was where it said it would be. Your daughter has a watch." Ada climbed down, and Joss looked first at her case, then at her coat, then at her face. "You've come," he said. Ada removed one glove, though there was no hand extended to take hers. "Yes."

Mara waited, and the pony breathed into the pause. "The west room's damp," Joss said at last. "Then open the window," Mara told him, leaning across the seat. He took the case before Ada could stop him; his right hand closed on the handle, and the left hovered beneath it, not quite able to help. The case knocked once against his shin, and he carried it indoors as though it had not.

Mara leaned toward Ada. "I'll bring the rest of the post tomorrow. And lamp oil, since apparently I've begun lying for him in practical ways." Ada looked at the place on her wrist where Mara had gripped her and made herself answer before the cart could turn. "Thank you for coming to meet me." "There. The mainland taught you something useful after all." Mara squeezed Ada's wrist, climbed back into the cart, and turned the pony in a circle that sent gravel against the rain barrel, while Joss did not come back to the door.

Inside, the keeper's house smelled of paraffin and stone that never quite dried. Ada's case stood at the foot of the stairs, one corner turned toward the wall where Joss had set it down rather than carried it farther, and on the kitchen table were a heel of bread, a covered dish, and the lighthouse logbook. The book lay square with the table edge; beside it, a pen had made two small blots on the cloth, unequal circles joined by the lightest dragged line. Joss filled the kettle from a jug. "We had tea at Mara's," Ada said. "This is for the lamp room." He set the kettle on the range and moved the covered dish half an inch to the left, withdrawing his left hand only after a tremor had passed from the wrist into the fingers and disturbed the cloth beneath the dish. Ada had seen such tremors in old men paying bills at the stationer's, some slight, some violent, all made worse by being watched, and she knew the courtesy of pretending to study a receipt until the coins had been counted; she looked at the logbook instead, paying for the courtesy by not crossing the room to take the kettle from him.

The day's entries ran down the ruled page in her father's hand. Six in the morning: wind west by north, light rain, visibility four miles. Noon: wind west, clear, visibility eight miles. The first line was clean; in the second, the downstroke of the g had been retraced, and the figure eight pressed through to the sheet beneath, leaving a white ridge where the nib had forced the paper rather than moved across it. "You haven't fetched your post," she said. Joss checked the lid of the dented can waiting by the range and replied, "Nothing needed." Ada rested two fingers beside the noon entry. "My letter is there." "You were coming whether I read it or not." The page shifted under her hand as she said, "I said I would stay a month."

Joss lifted the kettle before it boiled and poured the water into the can. "We'll see what the weather does." Steam passed over his closed left hand, and Ada moved the logbook away from the damp before answering, "The weather isn't why I'm here." His right hand stopped on the can's wire handle; for a moment the only sound was the low complaint of the range, its iron contracting around a fire that needed stirring. "No," he said. "It isn't." He carried the hot water through the passage toward the tower, holding the can away from his leg and setting his feet carefully at the threshold. Ada took off her coat, folded it over the chair, picked up the logbook and pen, and followed, because the thing he had admitted was still in the room even after he left it.

The tower stairs turned tightly around a central iron post. Ada remembered climbing them on all fours, placing her hands where her father's boots had been, though there could not have been room for both in the memory; perhaps she had rebuilt the climb from later journeys until his boots and her hands occupied the same tread, one duty laid over the next without erasing it. At the first landing stood the oil cans, four full and one in use, each marked with its weight in chalk. At the second was a shelf of cleaning cloths and the curved wick scissors in a leather loop. A keeper used their bent blades to cut away the charred edge without reaching over the flame, and when Joss passed them without taking them, Ada noticed the omission more sharply than a tool left in the wrong place: his hand went toward the loop, stopped, and continued upward empty. In the lantern room, glass surrounded them on every side, while the lamp stood within its tall lens, brass below and prisms above, smaller in daylight than its duty. Joss set down the can and began the evening work in the order Ada remembered—polish the chimney, strain the oil, test the vent, trim the wicks, wind the clockwork weight—and as one task led into the next, she understood why he had written about wind and walls rather than hands: a clean flame required no guesswork, each part either had been done or had not, whereas a hand could fail for one cut and then hold steady long enough to make the failure seem invented.

Ada watched him polish the inside of the chimney, his cloth moving in short, forceful circles until twice the glass rang under his fingers. "Give me that," she said. Joss turned the chimney to inspect it against the western light. "It's nearly clean." Ada set the logbook safely beneath the south window and held out both hands. "Then it should be easy to give me." He passed her the chimney; a grey crescent remained along its upper rim, and she wiped it away, using both hands, before setting the glass on the folded cloth. Joss unhooked the wick scissors, fitting his fingers through the loops without looking at her. "How long?" she asked.

"How long what?" He opened the blades, and the metal points trembled against the brass tray. Ada put one hand flat on the worktable. "Since your hands began to shake." "They don't shake all day." The qualification gave her something measurable to refuse, and she waited until he looked up before repeating, "How long?" He fitted his fingers more deeply through the scissor loops; the blades closed a quarter inch above the blackened cloth and opened again, the failed cut leaving the wick unchanged while his thumb whitened against the handle. "Since March," he said. "Worse since August. The right is better in the morning. The left is no use for fine work. I can fill the oil reservoir and wind the lens weight, but I can't always get the char even. An uneven wick smokes, the chimney clouds, and the light loses distance. There. That's the account."

It was six sentences, Ada noted, and more than he had written her on the subject in seven months, though what held her was not the number but the sequence by which he had made the confession bearable: first a date, then the division of labour between his hands, then the tasks still possible, and only at the end the distance the light might lose. She heard herself say, "You should have told me in March." Joss laid the heel of his right hand against the burner. "You had work." Ada picked up the cleaning cloth and folded the grey part inward. "I have work now." "Yes." The scissors shook between them, not much, but enough that the points drew two small arcs over the black crust without touching it; Ada held out her hand and said, "Show me the proper angle." Joss did not surrender them at once. He looked past her to the sea, where the late light lay flat and metallic out to the horizon, then placed the scissors in her palm as carefully as if the sharpness rather than the need had delayed him.

"Not straight across," he said. "A little crown in the middle. Take only the crust. If you cut into the sound cloth, it will burn low until you wind it up." The first wick took Ada three attempts: she cut too little, and Joss pointed with one stiff finger; on the second she left a black thread at the edge; by the third, the char fell onto the brass tray in one thin piece, holding the curve of the wick for an instant before it broke. The work required less strength than keeping a column of figures aligned, but the same refusal to hurry, because a cut made in impatience could not be added back and because the line that looked level while her hand blocked the light showed a rise as soon as she moved away. "Again," Joss said. She trimmed the next, feeling him follow the blades rather than her hand, and he turned the burner for her slowly, keeping his hand on the heavier wheel where the tremor mattered less. When the edges were even, she replaced the chimney and fastened its catch, then looked from the tower clock to her watch. "Six minutes past lighting time," she said. Joss wiped the loose char into the tray. "Five by the tower clock." Ada listened to the familiar tick, which arrived a fraction after the second hand on her watch. "The tower clock loses forty seconds a day." "Thirty-two. I timed it Tuesday." Ada looked at him; his beard hid most of his mouth, but not the movement at one corner, and she allowed herself to correct the account in his favour. "Then we are only three minutes late," she said. "A scandal."

He touched the taper to the wicks, and flame travelled around the burner, blue at first, then yellow and steady. Together they lowered the chimney and raised the flame a fraction at a time, Joss watching for smoke while Ada watched his hand on the control, neither naming which sign would decide that the work had passed from him to her. When the lamp burned clean, the lens gathered it and sent its first pale reach over the water, across the rocks and the drowned road and the empty reach beyond. Below them, the causeway had vanished; only the marker posts remained, a diminishing row of black strokes between island and mainland, all Ada's counted intervals filled. She could see the post-house lamp in the town and, after a moment, a smaller green light at the ferry landing. Mara's port lamp, paid for in part by Joss, was exactly where it ought to be, its steadiness dependent on oil he pretended not to provide and care Mara pretended to resent. Ada kept her hand near the control until the lens carried the beam away and brought it back clean.

"You can take the first watch," Joss said. "I'll lie down until ten." Ada checked the oil level against the mark on the reservoir before asking, "And after ten?" Joss put the used cloth with the others. "We'll see." Ada straightened, blocking his reach for the logbook without having planned to. "No. We have seen. I'll take the night." He began to object, drawing breath and shifting his right hand toward the heavier wheel as if another task might still be found there, but the left hand had closed over the right again. Ada waited without helping him find a different subject, and when he finally said, "I can't do it alone," the admission left his hands with nothing to conceal.

The words were plain and did not improve by repetition. Ada turned to the lamp, where one edge of the flame had risen above the others, and adjusted it down until the crown was level, using the smallest movement the control allowed. "Tonight isn't alone," she said. Joss nodded once. He showed her how far the oil should fall before she filled it, where the spare matches were kept, and which pane of glass rattled in a north wind, putting each instruction in the order she would need it and laying his finger beside each mark without pretending his hand was still. Then he went down the turning stair. His steps were careful and evenly spaced, and Ada counted twenty-seven before the tower door closed, listening to the gaps as closely as to the footfalls in case one lasted too long.

Ada set the logbook on a small desk beneath the south window. Its page was ruled into columns for time, wind, weather, visibility, oil used, and remarks, and on the shelf below it stood older volumes in a row, their years lettered on the spines in her father's clean hand. She counted twenty-three. The earliest covered the year before the *Wren* went down; the next covered the year it did, its leather no different in colour or wear from those beside it, as if the shelf recognized only sequence and would offer no warning about which volume could alter the sum of everything after. Ada drew the current book closer instead and took the pen from its groove. At eight o'clock she entered: Wind west. Weather clear. Visibility eight miles. Light burning properly. Her figures were smaller than her father's and leaned less, leaving room beside them for another hand but no need for one while, outside, water covered the road in both directions and, inside, the lamp made its measured circle, each return finding Ada still at the desk with the pen uncapped and the next ruled line waiting.
