# Scene B — v3: Speech

The tower stairs turned tightly around a central iron post. At the first landing stood four full oil cans and one in use, each marked in chalk with its weight. At the second, folded cleaning cloths lay beneath the curved wick scissors in their leather loop. A keeper used the bent blades to trim the char without reaching over the flame. Joss's hand moved toward them, stopped short, and continued upward. Ada lifted the scissors from the loop and followed with them resting across the logbook.

In the lantern room, glass stood between them and the sea on every side. Joss set down the hot-water can and began the evening work in its old order: polish the chimney, strain the oil, test the vent, trim the wick, wind the clockwork weight. His cloth moved in short circles inside the chimney until the glass rang against his fingers. "Give me that," Ada said. He turned the chimney toward the western light. "It's nearly clean." She set the logbook under the south window and held out both hands. "Then it should be easy to give me." A grey crescent remained along the rim. She wiped it away, folded the cloth over the mark, and laid the clean glass on the worktable.

Joss looked at the empty leather loop. Ada placed the wick scissors between them. "How long?" He checked the oil strainer. "How long what?" Ada rested her fingertips on the brass tray. "Since your hands began to shake." He fitted his fingers through the scissor loops and said they did not shake all day. The points closed above the char without cutting it. Ada did not repeat the question. She watched the white pressure gather beneath his thumbnail until he said, "Since March. Worse since August. The right is better in the morning. The left is no use for fine work. I can fill the reservoir and wind the lens weight, but I can't always get the char even. An uneven wick smokes, the chimney clouds, and the light loses distance. There. That's the account."

Ada took the cloth she had just folded and opened it again. "No," she said. "That's the lamp's account, and I asked for yours. You wrote me about the west wall and the oil delivery and the rain working under the sill, because walls and oil and rain don't answer back. You gave me rheumatism after you had already broken one chimney, and when you broke another you let Mara decide how much truth could cross the water. I answered every letter as if accuracy made it complete, because that kept my work on one shore and yours on the other. I can say you should have told me in March, but I should have asked in April, and May, and every month after that when you used the weather to fill the page. I came here with a month written down as though need observed a notice period. I don't know whether I can keep the light, and I don't know what waits at the stationer's when the month is over. But I know I will not go back merely because the date I chose has arrived, and I will not help you call that weather."

Joss removed his fingers from the scissors. He turned them so the lower loop faced Ada and placed them in her open hand. Then he put one stiff forefinger beside the wick. "Not straight across," he said. "Leave a crown in the middle. Take only the crust. If you cut into the sound cloth, it'll burn low until you wind it up." Ada's first cut left a black thread at one edge; the second bent it without severing it; on the third, the char fell in a thin curved strip and broke against the brass tray. Joss rotated the burner with the heavier wheel while she trimmed the next wick. His palm held steady under the weight. When she finished, he turned the burner back for inspection and tapped once beside the edge she had left high. She corrected it without being told again.

Ada lowered the chimney and fastened its catch. Joss touched the taper to the wicks. Flame travelled around the burner, blue first and then yellow, while they raised it a fraction at a time. Smoke marked the glass; Ada lowered the control until the mark stopped. The lens gathered the flame and sent a pale bar over the water. Below, the causeway had vanished. Its nine marker posts remained above the tide, black strokes diminishing toward the mainland, and at the ferry landing a green light appeared, disappeared behind the turning frame, and returned.

"You can take the first watch," Joss said. "I'll lie down until ten." Ada checked the oil against the etched line. "And after ten?" He folded the used cloth with the others. "We'll see." She moved in front of the logbook. "No. We have seen. I'll take the night." His right hand went toward the heavy wheel although the work was finished; his left closed over it. He drew breath, held it through one passage of the beam, and said, "I can't do it alone."

Ada turned to the burner. One part of the flame had risen above the rest, and she adjusted it down with the smallest movement the control allowed. "Tonight isn't alone." Joss touched the filling mark on the reservoir, opened the drawer of spare matches, and pressed the pane that rattled in a north wind, giving her each instruction in the order she would need it. He no longer closed one hand over the other. Then he went down the turning stair. Ada counted twenty-seven steps before the tower door shut, attending to the spaces between them as closely as to the footfalls.
