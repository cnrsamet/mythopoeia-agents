# Chapter One

The tide table said twenty minutes to three. At thirty-three minutes past two, black stone showed between the mainland and Saltreach, first in separate crowns, then in a joined road. Ada set her case above the tide line and kept her glove around its handle. Water slid east across the stones. Her watch ticked against the pulse in her wrist, seven minutes ahead of the printed figure, and she did not step down.

The table in the glass-fronted box had swollen at its corners. Its columns were narrow, and the afternoon ebb stood between morning and evening in clean black type. Beside the box, salt had opened grey scales in the warning board. On the island marker, a white iron ring shone through brush marks, with water touching its underside. Ada rubbed her thumb along the case handle until the leather warmed. Beyond the ring, two rows of slate roofs caught the afternoon; the western rise hid the lighthouse.

At forty-one minutes past, a pony cart rolled between the houses and stopped at the far marker. The driver stood and whistled. Wind tore the answering shout flat across the water, but the scarf over the driver's hair held its red. The cart came on, iron rims knocking at the joints, the pony's fetlocks dark to the bone. When it drew near, the woman in the man's oilskin had Mara Quill's scarf, Mara's square hands, and a pale seam across one knuckle that the blunt knife on the old net-shed block had left when two girls cut stolen wool in half. Mara had wrapped the knuckle in one red piece and tied the other around Ada's wrist. Now the scarf ends struck her oilskin collar.

The left wheel stopped in a runnel. Mara looked at Ada, then at the case. "That's a small case for twenty-one years." "Four shirts, two skirts, and my work clothes." Mara nudged the postbag away. "Bookkeepers need a uniform now?" "Only for dangerous sums." Mara's laugh made the pony toss its head. She climbed down and drew her against cold oilcloth. Ada's gloves were caught between their ribs. The case buckle knocked her shin. When Mara let go, she took the case and heaved it into the cart. "Get in before the road goes greasy under you."

Tarred rope lay beneath the seat. Its fibres had stiffened into hooks. Three flour sacks filled the crate behind them. Mara turned when the white ring stood clear by the width of her finger. The pony stepped onto wet stone, shoes striking, slipping, striking again. Ada put her case beside the rope and braced one boot against it. Nine marker posts stood where six had been. She counted each black band and the spaces between, touching thumb to fingertip under her gloves.

At the fourth marker, Ada laid one glove over the other. "He said it was rheumatism." Mara shortened the right rein. "It may be." "You wrote that he dropped the lamp chimney." Mara drew the pony around a stone with one corner broken away. "He dropped two. I only told you about one." The wheel entered a hollow and brown water slapped the floorboard. Ada lifted her heels. "Why?" Mara ran the rein through her palm and pointed the pony toward the crown of the road. "After the first, you sent back his own words. I wasn't posting them a second time."

Ada folded the gloves along their old seams. Her thumbnail caught the worn piping at the forefinger and pulled it crooked. She flattened it, opened both gloves, and began again. Mara kept the pony between the wheel tracks until the sixth marker passed behind them; there she set the reins across one thigh and retied the scarf, tugging the knot once beneath her ear. The pale seam on her knuckle disappeared into the red wool. "I wrote every month for the first year after you went," she said. "You answered twice. Tom died, then Lena, then you went, and you left no word for me at all. Now I send four lines about Joss and you cross in a week. Don't climb into my cart and call that last Tuesday. You left without saying goodbye to me." Ada stopped smoothing the gloves. She laid them on the flour sacks, reached across the postbag, and pulled the loose end of Mara's scarf free where the knot had trapped it. She folded the end back once and tucked it flat beneath the knot, covering the old white seam in Mara's hand. Then she put her case lengthwise between their boots instead of at the edge of the cart. Mara picked up the reins. "That will do." At the seventh marker she pointed her chin toward three muddy fields and said the turnips had formed a government. Ada's mouth opened before the laugh came; Mara's came at once, broad enough to send the pony's ears back, and the two sounds travelled with the cart toward the houses.

By the ninth marker, the town had resolved into particular houses, each roof separating from the next and each chimney taking its own angle against the sky. Ada knew the post by the blue enamel sign beside its door, though the lower room had once been Tom Quill's net shed, and a ferry waited at the stone landing beyond it, painted green and white, broad enough for six passengers if four were friendly. Mara followed Ada's look. "She runs better than she looks," she said. "Unlike her owner." Ada examined the line of the ferry's gunwale, which rose a little at the bow but sat even against the landing, and asked, "Is she yours?" Mara loosened the reins as the pony found the dry road. "Mine and the bank's. Your father pays for the ferry's lamp oil, though I've told him the post can afford it. He says a boat ought to show where it is." Ada looked back at the westward road, but the lighthouse was still hidden by the rise, so all she could see of her father's care was a green-painted boat with a lamp not yet lit. "He likes a light accounted for." Mara's mouth changed, not quite closing, and her hand lifted as if she meant to rub at it; then she flicked the reins and called the pony a scholar for stepping over a rut.

They passed the forty houses slowly. Ada did not count them, because she knew there had been forty when she left and did not want to discover that memory had kept bad books. A woman at the bakehouse door raised a floury hand, and two men mending pots outside the inn stopped their work; Ada had prepared for faces she could not name, but not for people who knew hers. At the road that climbed west, Mara stopped the cart. "Post first," she said. "Then I'll take you up." Ada reached for her case. "I can walk."

Mara rested one hand on the brake. "You can. You'll arrive cross and wet, and Joss will say that proves the mainland has made you foolish." Ada put both feet on the road but left one hand on the case. "What does my arriving in your cart prove?" Mara gathered the postbag into her lap. "That it has made you teachable. Leave the case. Come in while I sort this lot." The case stayed among three sacks of flour while Ada followed Mara beneath the enamel sign.

The post occupied the front room of Mara's house, and pigeonholes covered one wall, each labelled in the same blunt capitals—BELL, CROWE, FENN, QUILL, VANE—with no need for street names on an island with forty houses. Mara tipped the postbag onto the counter and put a kettle on the little iron stove; Ada stood opposite her and sorted without being asked: nine letters, four bills, a seed catalogue, two newspapers three days old, and a parcel that smelled strongly of soap. Her hands knew the work before the room. They straightened the envelopes, turned the stamps the same way, and separated demands from personal letters by the quality of paper, until the years between this counter and the mainland stationer's desk seemed no more important than the difference between two ledgers. Mara objected only when Ada placed the bills together. "If you give people all their bad news at once, they blame the postmistress. Mix in a letter from a nephew."

Ada held a bill for Crowe in one hand and a thin envelope in the other. "That alters the order, not the total." Mara took both from her and slid the letter on top. "It alters the walk home." The kettle took too long, so Mara found biscuits in three tins. One batch was burnt, one soft, and one tasted of onions; they ate the onion ones after Mara tried one and looked offended enough that Ada had to test the evidence.

Mara poured the tea dark and pushed the least chipped cup across the counter. "Your room at the light has been shut since Lena died. I opened the window last week. Joss shut it again." Ada put down her cup with the handle aligned to the saucer's faded flower; no one on the mainland said her mother's name unless Ada said it first, and she seldom did, so hearing it in the room where Lena Vane had once collected letters left Ada with nowhere prepared to look. "He knew I was coming." Mara rubbed a crumb from the counter into her palm and said, "Knowing isn't the same as letting air in. Your mother used to say that about him." Ada's fingers tightened on the cup handle, though the tea was too hot to lift. "What else did she say?" Mara looked at her then, fully and without the shelter of a joke. "That he'd mistake silence for strength until something broke under it. She loved him. She was tired of him. Those aren't enemies either."

In the pigeonhole marked VANE lay the note she had posted three days before leaving. Joss had not come down for it. Mara saw where Ada was looking and said, "He hasn't fetched the post since Monday." Ada calculated the days. "Today is Thursday." Mara dropped the last onion biscuit into its tin. "I can count to three." Ada finished her tea, and the cup clicked against the saucer; she kept her fingers on it until it was still, then turned the envelope face down before helping Mara put the remaining letters into the pigeonholes. She could not improve the fact that it had waited there, but she could keep her handwriting from looking at her.

The road to the western point rose between low stone walls. Mara drove, the town dropped behind them roof by roof, and the wind returned with nothing to stop it, bending the field grass steadily east. At the top of the rise the lighthouse appeared all at once: a white tower joined to a square keeper's house, set above a black descent of rock, less tall than the tower Ada had carried away in memory and more exposed, every wall showing what salt had done to its paint. Beyond it, the sea had no sheltered colour. Far below the headland, the Teeth were beginning to uncover as the tide withdrew, dark points in a ragged line, and Ada looked away before she had counted them, but their order remained behind her eyes: the first low enough for most waves to cover, the next shouldering through foam, the farther ones leading toward the water where the *Wren* had gone down. She did not permit herself the number. Numbers belonged to things that could be checked, entered, and carried forward, while this one had been told to her in lowered voices until it included the men on the ship, the people on shore, the years before her birth, and her father's silence after her mother's death, a total altered whenever someone refused to name one of its parts. She fixed on the keeper's house instead, counting two chimney pots, three western windows, the rain barrel, and one man waiting in a blue coat.

Joss Vane wore his keeper's coat despite the mild afternoon. At sixty-seven he was narrower than he had been in Ada's memory. His beard was white around the mouth and clipped evenly, and he held one hand inside the other. Mara drew up beside the rain barrel. "Delivery for you. Slightly delayed."

Joss kept his eyes on the cart. "Tide was late," he said. Mara set the brake. "Tide was where it said it would be. Your daughter has a watch." Ada climbed down, and Joss looked at her case, then at her face. "You've come," he said. Ada removed one glove, though there was no hand extended. "Yes."

Mara waited, and the pony breathed into the pause. "The west room's damp," Joss said. "Then open the window," Mara told him. He took the case before Ada could stop him; his right hand closed on the handle, and the left hovered beneath it. The case knocked against his shin, and he carried it indoors as though it had not.

Mara leaned toward Ada. "I'll bring the post tomorrow. And lamp oil, since apparently I've begun lying for him in practical ways." Ada made herself answer before the cart could turn. "Thank you for coming to meet me." "There. The mainland taught you something useful after all." Mara squeezed Ada's wrist, climbed into the cart, and turned the pony while Joss did not come back to the door.

Inside, the keeper's house smelled of paraffin and stone that never quite dried. Ada's case stood at the foot of the stairs, one corner turned toward the wall where Joss had set it down rather than carried it farther, and on the kitchen table were a heel of bread, a covered dish, and the lighthouse logbook. The book lay square with the table edge; beside it, a pen had made two small blots on the cloth, unequal circles joined by the lightest dragged line. Joss filled the kettle from a jug. "We had tea at Mara's," Ada said. "This is for the lamp room." He set the kettle on the range and moved the covered dish half an inch to the left, withdrawing his left hand only after a tremor had passed from the wrist into the fingers and disturbed the cloth beneath the dish. Ada had seen such tremors in old men paying bills at the stationer's, some slight, some violent, all made worse by being watched, and she knew the courtesy of pretending to study a receipt until the coins had been counted; she looked at the logbook instead, paying for the courtesy by not crossing the room to take the kettle from him.

The day's entries ran down the ruled page in her father's hand. Six in the morning: wind west by north, light rain, visibility four miles. Noon: wind west, clear, visibility eight miles. The first line was clean; in the second, the downstroke of the g had been retraced, and the figure eight pressed through to the sheet beneath. "You haven't fetched your post," she said. Joss checked the dented can waiting by the range and replied, "Nothing needed." Ada rested two fingers beside the noon entry. "My letter is there." "You were coming whether I read it or not." The page shifted under her hand. "I said I would stay a month."

Joss lifted the kettle before it boiled and poured the water into the can. "We'll see what the weather does." Steam passed over his closed left hand. Ada moved the logbook away from the damp. "The weather isn't why I'm here." His right hand stopped on the can's wire handle. "No," he said. "It isn't." He carried the hot water toward the tower, holding the can away from his leg and setting his feet carefully at the threshold. Ada took off her coat, folded it over the chair, picked up the logbook and pen, and followed, because the thing he had admitted was still in the room.

The tower stairs turned around a central iron post. At the first landing stood four full oil cans and one in use, each marked in chalk with its weight. At the second, cleaning cloths lay beneath the curved wick scissors in their leather loop. A keeper used the bent blades to trim the char without reaching over the flame. Joss's hand moved toward them, stopped short, and continued upward. Ada lifted the scissors from the loop and followed with them across the logbook.

In the lantern room, glass stood between them and the sea on every side. Joss began the evening work in its old order: polish the chimney, strain the oil, test the vent, trim the wick, wind the clockwork weight. His cloth moved in short circles until the glass rang against his fingers. "Give me that," Ada said. He turned the chimney toward the light. "It's nearly clean." She held out both hands. "Then it should be easy to give me." A grey crescent remained along the rim. She wiped it away, folded the cloth over the mark, and laid the clean glass on the worktable.

Joss looked at the empty leather loop. Ada placed the wick scissors between them. "How long?" He checked the oil strainer. "How long what?" "Since your hands began to shake." He fitted his fingers through the scissor loops and said they did not shake all day. The points closed above the char without cutting it. Ada watched the white pressure gather beneath his thumbnail until he said, "Since March. Worse since August. The right is better in the morning. The left is no use for fine work. I can fill the reservoir and wind the lens weight, but I can't always get the char even. An uneven wick smokes, the chimney clouds, and the light loses distance. There. That's the account."

Ada took the cloth she had just folded and opened it again. "No," she said. "That's the lamp's account, and I asked for yours. You wrote me about the west wall and the oil delivery and the rain working under the sill, because walls and oil and rain don't answer back. You gave me rheumatism after you had already broken one chimney, and when you broke another you let Mara decide how much truth could cross the water. I answered every letter as if accuracy made it complete, because that kept my work on one shore and yours on the other. I can say you should have told me in March, but I should have asked in April, and May, and every month after that when you used the weather to fill the page. I came here with a month written down as though need observed a notice period. I don't know whether I can keep the light, and I don't know what waits at the stationer's when the month is over. But I know I will not go back merely because the date I chose has arrived, and I will not help you call that weather."

Joss removed his fingers from the scissors. He placed them in her open hand. Then he put one stiff forefinger beside the wick. "Not straight across," he said. "Leave a crown in the middle. Take only the crust. If you cut into the sound cloth, it'll burn low until you wind it up." Ada's first cut left a black thread; the second bent it without severing it; on the third, the char fell in a curved strip. Joss rotated the burner with the heavier wheel while she trimmed the next wick. His palm held steady under the weight. When she finished, he tapped once beside the edge she had left high. She corrected it.

Ada lowered the chimney and fastened its catch. Joss touched the taper to the wicks. Flame travelled around the burner, blue then yellow, while they raised it. Smoke marked the glass; Ada lowered the control until the mark stopped. The lens sent a pale bar over the water. Below, the causeway had vanished. Its nine marker posts remained above the tide, and at the ferry landing a green light appeared, disappeared behind the turning frame, and returned.

"You can take the first watch," Joss said. "I'll lie down until ten." Ada checked the oil. "And after ten?" "We'll see." She moved in front of the logbook. "No. We have seen. I'll take the night." His right hand went toward the heavy wheel; his left closed over it. He drew breath, held it through one passage of the beam, and said, "I can't do it alone."

Ada turned to the burner. One part of the flame had risen, and she adjusted it down. "Tonight isn't alone." Joss touched the filling mark, opened the drawer of matches, and pressed the pane that rattled in a north wind, giving each instruction in order. He no longer closed one hand over the other. Then he went down the stair. Ada counted twenty-seven steps before the tower door shut, attending to the spaces between them as closely as to the footfalls.

Ada set the logbook on a small desk beneath the south window. Its page was ruled into columns for time, wind, weather, visibility, oil used, and remarks, and on the shelf below it stood older volumes in a row, their years lettered on the spines in her father's clean hand. She counted twenty-three. The earliest covered the year before the *Wren* went down; the next covered the year it did, its leather no different in colour or wear from those beside it, as if the shelf recognized only sequence and would offer no warning about which volume could alter the sum of everything after. Ada drew the current book closer instead and took the pen from its groove. At eight o'clock she entered: Wind west. Weather clear. Visibility eight miles. Light burning properly. Her figures were smaller than her father's and leaned less, leaving room beside them for another hand but no need for one while, outside, water covered the road in both directions and, inside, the lamp made its measured circle, each return finding Ada still at the desk with the pen uncapped and the next ruled line waiting.
