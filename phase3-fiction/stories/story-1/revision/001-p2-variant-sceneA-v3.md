# Scene A — v3: Speech

The tide table said the causeway would be passable at twenty minutes to three. At thirty-three minutes past two, Ada Vane watched black stone rise between mainland and Saltreach, one crown after another, but kept her hand on the case and waited. Her watch and the printed column agreed. Water still ran over the wheel tracks, and neither watch nor table would put a boot down for her. Across the channel, the island marker showed its white ring by slow degrees; behind it two rows of roofs caught the afternoon, while the western rise kept the lighthouse and Joss out of sight.

At forty-one minutes past, a pony cart came down from the houses. The driver stood, whistled with two fingers, and brought the cart onto the causeway as soon as the ring cleared. Ada knew Mara Quill by the red scarf before she knew the broad figure inside the man's oilskin. When they were girls they had cut one such scarf in half on the net-shed block, pulling the wool against a blunt knife until the edges came away crooked. Ada's half remained beneath receipts in a biscuit tin on the mainland. Mara's appeared to have gone on being worn.

The cart stopped with one wheel in running water. Mara looked down at the case. "That's a small case for twenty-one years." Ada kept hold of it. "Four shirts, two skirts, and my work clothes." Mara pushed the postbag aside with her boot. "Bookkeepers have work clothes?" "For dangerous sums." Mara laughed once, climbed down, and closed cold oilskin around Ada's shoulders. Ada held her gloves between them. When Mara released her, she took the case and put it beside three flour sacks and a coil of tarred rope.

They started back over the wet stones. The pony chose its own footing while Ada counted nine marker posts, then counted the intervals to make sure. At the fourth she folded her gloves. "He said it was rheumatism." Mara's hands stayed level on the reins. "It may be." "You wrote that he dropped the lamp chimney." Mara drew the cart around a broken stone. "He dropped two." Ada turned her gloves over. "Why did you tell me one?"

Mara waited until the wheel had climbed out of a hollow. Then she laid the reins across her thigh and spoke without looking at Ada. "Because after the first, you wrote back that he called it nothing, and I wasn't going to carry his answer over the water twice. Because you and Joss can make a silence look neat enough to put in an envelope, and both of you trust neat things more than people who have to stand beside the breakage. When Tom died, you stood beside me at the burial with your hands flat at your sides, and I took that for all the words we would need. Then Lena died, then you left, and I wrote you every month for a year; you answered twice, and one of those was to correct the ferry day. I was angry when I wrote about the chimney. I was glad when you answered. I am both now, and you'll have to sit beside both of them because there isn't another cart coming. Don't tell me you meant to write, don't tell me the stationer couldn't spare you, and don't tell me twenty-one years went quickly. You left without saying goodbye to me, Ada, and I minded it every time the post came over with your hand on an envelope for somebody else."

The gloves lay half folded in Ada's lap. She opened them, placed one palm over the other, and pressed until the old creases crossed instead of meeting. At the sixth marker she put them aside. She reached down, lifted the coil of the ferry's spare painter away from her case, and set the case square between her boots, where she would have to keep it steady for the rest of the crossing. Mara picked up the reins again. Ada did not offer an account of the two replies or the card. She held the case while the pony stepped over the seventh band of weed and, when Mara said Saltreach had been conquered by a damned multitude of turnips in her absence, bent forward with a laugh that knocked her shoulder against Mara's. Mara did not move away.
