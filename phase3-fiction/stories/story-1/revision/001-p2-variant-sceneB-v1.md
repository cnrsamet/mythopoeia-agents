# Scene B — v1: Inner Development

The tower stairs turned around a central iron post, each tread narrowing toward it until Ada had to decide whether to place her foot where the tread was broad or where the rail gave her something to hold. She remembered climbing there on all fours behind Joss's boots, although there could not have been room for his boots and her hands in the same instant; memory had kept the order of dependence and discarded the measurements, then rebuilt the scene until his weight and hers occupied one tread. At the first landing stood four full oil cans and one in use, their weights chalked on the sides. At the second, cleaning cloths were stacked beneath the curved wick scissors in their leather loop. A keeper used the bent blades to remove the char without reaching over the flame. Joss's right hand moved toward them, stopped, and followed the rest of him upward. Ada watched the empty hand rise. His letters had recorded the damp in the west wall, the oil delivered, the glass replaced, and the number of clear nights; they had given her every part of the light except the hand maintaining it, as if duty were a sum from which the person doing it could be omitted without changing the total. That was how omission acquired the look of duty.

In the lantern room, sea and sky met in every pane. The lamp stood inside its tall lens, brass below and prisms above, smaller than the sweep it would make after dark. Joss set down the can and began in the remembered order: chimney, oil, vent, wick, weight. Ada had believed the sequence belonged to him, but now she saw that he belonged to it, each task carrying him to the next before either of them could ask whether the hands were fit. The cloth made short, hard circles inside the chimney. Twice the glass rang. She thought of the two broken chimneys Mara had counted and the one Joss had converted into rheumatism, then of the letters she had answered by accepting the conversion because a named complaint asked nothing except acknowledgment. Seven months separated March from the present afternoon. She could divide them into rent days, stock counts, and Sundays spent walking the same mainland streets, yet could not find the date on which she ought to have understood what had not been written. The omission had done seven months of work.

"Give me that," she said. Joss turned the chimney toward the western light. "It's nearly clean." Ada put the logbook beneath the south window and held out both hands. "Then it should be easy to give me." He passed it across. A grey crescent remained along the upper rim; she wiped until it vanished, folded the dirty part of the cloth inward, and set the chimney down. Joss took the wick scissors from their loop. "How long?" Ada asked. He opened the blades above the brass tray. Their points trembled. "How long what?" "Since your hands began to shake." He said they did not shake all day, and she heard in the qualification the same method she used when a tenant paid five shillings of ten and asked that the entry show payment rather than debt. She waited. The blades closed above the char and opened without cutting. "Since March," he said. "Worse since August. The right is better in the morning. The left is no use for fine work. I can fill the reservoir and wind the lens weight, but I can't always get the char even. An uneven wick smokes, the chimney clouds, and the light loses distance. There. That's the account."

Six sentences: a date, a decline, the division of labour between his hands, the remaining tasks, the failure, the consequence. He had arranged the truth so the light came last and his body could appear only as its defective instrument. Ada thought of the morning her mother died, when Joss had written the hour on a scrap torn from the back of the log because the doctor asked him twice, and of the evening afterward, when he lit the lamp at the proper time. She had taken the regular beam for steadiness. Later she had taken his short letters for strength, her own short replies for respect, and the distance between them for the unavoidable result of work on two shores. Each interpretation had preserved them from making a demand; each had also preserved the distance. She had come intending to inspect his hands and calculate a stay, as if his need were damage in a rented room and her life the estimate for repair, but the hand holding the scissors was also the hand that had buttoned her coat badly when her mother was no longer there to do it, missing one hole and beginning again without speaking. She had come to count, and found herself counted.

"You should have told me in March," she said. Joss put the heel of his right hand against the burner. "You had work." Ada folded the cloth once more although no clean square remained. "I have work now." The scissors drew two small arcs over the black wick. She held out her hand. "Show me the proper angle." Joss looked through the glass toward the flat metallic sea before he placed the scissors in her palm. He told her not to cut straight across, to leave a crown, to take only the crust. The first wick needed three attempts. On the third, the char came away in a thin curve and broke against the tray. He turned the heavy burner wheel while she trimmed the next, his hand steadier under weight than over fine work. When the edges were even, she replaced the chimney, and together they brought the flame from blue to yellow, raised it until smoke touched the glass, then lowered it by a fraction.

The lens sent its first pale reach across the water. The causeway had disappeared, leaving only marker posts between the island and mainland, and Ada saw how easily a road could remain in its exact place while becoming unusable. She could take the next crossing and return to the stationer; she could write to the lighthouse board, arrange a relief keeper, send money Joss would return, and preserve every existing column by adding one more. The available solutions passed through her mind with the tidy speed of practiced sums. None required her to stand where she stood. That was their recommendation and their failure. The green ferry light showed below, steady on the landing, paid for by Joss without admission and tended by Mara without thanks. Ada rested her fingers beside the flame control. She could no longer enter departure as the only clean figure.

"You can take the first watch," Joss said. "I'll lie down until ten." Ada checked the oil against the mark. "And after ten?" He put away the cloth. "We'll see." Ada stepped between him and the logbook. "No. We have seen. I'll take the night." His breath entered as if to begin an objection, but his left hand had closed over his right. When he said, "I can't do it alone," Ada adjusted the high edge of the flame until the crown stood level. "Tonight isn't alone." Joss nodded. He showed her the filling mark, the spare matches, and the pane that rattled in a north wind, touching each place in the order she would need it. Then he descended. Ada counted twenty-seven careful steps before the door closed, including the pauses between them in case one became too long.
