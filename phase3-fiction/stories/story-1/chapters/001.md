# Chapter One

The tide table said the causeway would be passable at twenty minutes to three. At thirty-three minutes past two, Ada Vane could already see the black crowns of the middle stones, one after another, between the mainland and Saltreach. She stood beside her case and watched the water draw itself off the road. Seven minutes early was not early enough to trust.

She had come back to see how much her father had left out of his letters. She meant to reach the lighthouse before dark, look at his hands, and decide how long she would have to stay. The first two purposes were plain. The third had occupied most of the train journey without producing a figure.

The causeway keeper had been gone for years; there was only a board now, grey with salt, warning strangers not to cross until the marker at the island end stood clear to its iron ring. Ada remembered a red ring. This one had been painted white, though badly, and the water still touched its lower edge. Beyond it the town showed as two uneven rows of slate roofs on the sheltered shore. The lighthouse did not show. It stood on the other side of the island, where the land turned its back on the town.

At forty-one minutes past, a pony cart came down between the houses and stopped at the marker. The driver stood up, put two fingers to her mouth, and whistled across half a mile of wet stone.

Ada lifted one hand.

The driver shouted something the wind took away. Then she sat down, slapped the reins against the pony's broad back, and brought the cart onto the causeway.

Mara Quill had still worn her hair in a long plait when Ada last saw her and was now a large woman in a man's oilskin, with a postbag wedged against one boot and a red scarf tied over her hair. Ada knew the scarf first. Mara had worn one like it as a girl, stolen from her father's drawer and cut in half so they could each have a piece. Ada's half was in a biscuit tin in a room on the mainland, though she had not thought of it in years.

The cart halted with its left wheel in a runnel of seawater. Mara looked down at Ada, then at the case.

"That's a small case for twenty-one years," she said.

"Four shirts, two skirts, and my work clothes."

"Have they made bookkeepers wear special clothes now?"

"Only when the sums are dangerous."

Mara gave a single loud laugh. It startled the pony into lifting its head, and for a moment Ada could see the girl she had known, not in Mara's face but in the way the sound occupied more space than it needed. Mara climbed down and caught Ada around the shoulders. The oilskin was cold. Ada's hands remained between them, still holding her gloves, until Mara let go.

"Put your case in," Mara said. "Unless you've learned to carry luggage over weed without breaking your neck."

The mail cart smelled of tarred rope, damp wool, and apples. The rope lay in a neat coil under the seat, blackened against rot; it was the ferry's spare painter, Mara said, used to tie the boat at the landing. Ada set her case beside a crate holding three flour sacks and climbed up. Mara looked once at the white ring, clear now by a finger's breadth, before she turned the pony. It was the first thing about her that Ada recognized without effort.

For the first hundred yards the causeway stones were merely wet. Farther on, brown weed lay over them in ropes, and the pony chose its footing without guidance. Water hurried through the gaps on either side. Ada kept her eyes on the marker posts. There were nine now instead of the six she remembered, each with a black band showing the safe line. She counted them twice.

"He said it was rheumatism," she said when they passed the fourth.

Mara looked ahead. "It may be."

"You wrote that he had dropped the lamp chimney."

"He dropped two. I only told you about one."

"Why?"

"Because after the first you wrote back that he said it was nothing, and I knew what sort of arithmetic we were doing."

Ada folded her gloves along their old creases. Her father's letters came on the first ferry of every month. They contained the week's highest wind, the state of the west wall, and, since spring, brief complaints about the damp in his knuckles. Her replies contained the rent she collected for the stationer, the weather on the mainland, and money orders he returned untouched. Nothing in either set of letters had required a second page.

"You could have written plainly," Ada said.

Mara pulled the pony a little to the right of a broken stone. "I did. You're here."

At the sixth marker she spoke again, louder, though the wind had dropped. "I was glad when you answered. I was angry it took my letter to bring you, and I was glad all the same. Both can sit in the cart; there's room. I don't expect you to say you missed me, because you never did like owing anybody a sentence. But don't come back and make me pretend you only went away last Tuesday. You left, Ada. I minded it."

The white edges of Ada's gloves no longer met precisely. She unfolded them and began again. "I know."

"Good. That's the real thing said. We can discuss turnips now."

"Are there many?"

"A damned multitude. Saltreach is lost under them."

Mara grinned, and this time Ada laughed with her. The sound was smaller, but it crossed the same strip of water.

By the ninth marker, the town had resolved into particular houses. Ada knew the post by the blue enamel sign beside its door, though the lower room had once been Tom Quill's net shed. A ferry waited at the stone landing beyond it, painted green and white, broad enough for six passengers if four were friendly. Mara followed Ada's look.

"She runs better than she looks," she said. "Unlike her owner."

"Is she yours?"

"Mine and the bank's. Your father pays for the ferry's lamp oil, though I've told him the post can afford it. He says a boat ought to show where it is."

Ada looked back at the westward road, but the lighthouse was still hidden by the rise. "He likes a light accounted for."

Mara's mouth changed, not quite closing. Then she flicked the reins and called the pony a scholar for stepping over a rut.

They passed the forty houses slowly. Ada did not count them, because she knew there had been forty when she left and did not want to discover that memory had kept bad books. A woman at the bakehouse door raised a floury hand. Two men mending pots outside the inn stopped their work. Ada had prepared herself for faces she could not name, but not for people who knew hers.

At the road that climbed west, Mara stopped the cart.

"Post first," she said. "Then I'll take you up."

"I can walk."

"You can. You'll arrive cross and wet, and Joss will say that proves the mainland has made you foolish."

Ada reached for her case. "What does my arriving in your cart prove?"

"That it has made you teachable. Leave the case. Come in and drink something while I sort this lot."

The post occupied the front room of Mara's house. Pigeonholes covered one wall, each labelled in the same blunt capitals: BELL, CROWE, FENN, QUILL, VANE. There was no need for street names on an island with forty houses. Mara tipped the postbag onto the counter and put a kettle on the little iron stove. Ada stood opposite her and sorted without being asked: nine letters, four bills, a seed catalogue, two newspapers three days old, and a parcel that smelled strongly of soap. Mara objected only when Ada placed the bills together.

"If you give people all their bad news at once, they blame the postmistress. Mix in a letter from a nephew."

"That alters the order, not the total."

"It alters the walk home."

The kettle took too long on the damp stove. Mara filled the time by finding biscuits in three different tins, rejecting the contents of each, and finally setting all three before Ada. One batch was burnt, one soft, and one tasted faintly of onions. They ate the onion ones.

Mara poured the tea dark. "Your room at the light has been shut since Lena died. I opened the window last week. Joss shut it again."

Ada put down her cup. No one on the mainland said her mother's name unless Ada said it first, and she seldom did. "He knew I was coming."

"Knowing isn't the same as letting air in. Your mother used to say that about him."

"What else did she say?"

Mara looked at her then, fully and without the shelter of a joke. "That he'd mistake silence for strength until something broke under it. She loved him. She was tired of him. Those aren't enemies either."

In the pigeonhole marked VANE lay one envelope addressed in Ada's own hand. It was the note she had posted three days before leaving, though she had also sent a wire to the mainland inn. Joss had not come down for it.

Mara saw where she was looking. "He hasn't fetched the post since Monday."

"Today is Thursday."

"I can count to three."

Ada finished her tea. The cup clicked against the saucer, and she kept her fingers on it until it was still.

The road to the western point rose between low stone walls. Mara drove. The town dropped behind them, roof by roof, and the wind returned with nothing to stop it. In the fields the grass grew one way, flattened east. At the top of the rise the lighthouse appeared all at once: a white tower joined to a square keeper's house, the whole of it set above a black descent of rock. Beyond, the sea had no sheltered colour. Far below the headland, the Teeth were beginning to uncover as the tide withdrew, dark points in a ragged line. Ada looked away before she had counted them.

Joss Vane was waiting in the doorway. He wore his blue keeper's coat despite the mild afternoon. At sixty-seven he was narrower than he had been in Ada's memory, though perhaps memory had added the width a child required. His beard was white around the mouth and clipped as evenly as ever. He held one hand closed inside the other.

Mara drew up beside the rain barrel. "Delivery for you. Slightly delayed."

"Tide was late," Joss said.

"Tide was where it said it would be. Your daughter has a watch."

Ada climbed down. Joss looked first at her case, then at her coat, then at her face.

"You've come," he said.

"Yes."

Mara waited. The pony breathed into the pause.

"The west room's damp," Joss said at last.

"Then open the window," Mara told him.

He took the case before Ada could stop him. His right hand closed on the handle; the left hovered beneath it, not quite able to help. The case knocked once against his shin. He carried it indoors as though it had not.

Mara leaned toward Ada. "I'll bring the rest of the post tomorrow. And lamp oil, since apparently I've begun lying for him in practical ways."

"Thank you for coming to meet me."

"There. The mainland taught you something useful after all."

She squeezed Ada's wrist, climbed back into the cart, and turned the pony in a circle that sent gravel against the rain barrel. Joss had not come back to the door.

Inside, the keeper's house smelled of paraffin and stone that never quite dried. Ada's case stood at the foot of the stairs. On the kitchen table were a heel of bread, a covered dish, and the lighthouse logbook. The book lay square with the table edge. Beside it, a pen had made two small blots on the cloth.

Joss filled the kettle from a jug.

"We had tea at Mara's," Ada said.

"This is for the lamp room."

He set the kettle on the range and moved the covered dish half an inch to the left. His left hand trembled as he withdrew it. Ada had seen such tremors in old men paying bills at the stationer's: some slight, some violent, all made worse by being watched. She looked at the logbook instead.

The day's entries ran down the ruled page in her father's hand. Six in the morning: wind west by north, light rain, visibility four miles. Noon: wind west, clear, visibility eight miles. The first line was clean. In the second, the downstroke of the g had been retraced, and the figure eight pressed through to the sheet beneath.

"You haven't fetched your post," she said.

"Nothing needed."

"My letter is there."

"You were coming whether I read it or not."

"I said I would stay a month."

Joss lifted the kettle before it boiled and poured the water into a dented can. "We'll see what the weather does."

"The weather isn't why I'm here."

His hand stopped on the can's wire handle. For a moment the only sound was the low complaint of the range.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

He carried the hot water through the passage toward the tower. Ada took off her coat, folded it over the chair, picked up the logbook and pen, and followed.

The tower stairs turned tightly around a central iron post. She remembered climbing them on all fours, placing her hands where her father's boots had been, though there could not have been room for both of them in the memory. At the first landing stood the oil cans, four full and one in use, each marked with its weight in chalk. At the second was a shelf of cleaning cloths and the curved wick scissors in a leather loop. A keeper used their bent blades to cut away the charred edge without reaching over the flame. Joss passed the scissors without taking them.

In the lantern room, glass surrounded them on every side. The lamp stood at the centre within its tall lens, brass below and prisms above. In daylight it looked smaller than its duty. Joss set down the can and began the evening work in the order Ada remembered: polish the smoke from the chimney, strain the oil, test the vent, trim the wicks, wind the clockwork weight that turned the lens. A clean flame required no guesswork. Each part either had been done or had not.

Ada watched him polish the inside of the chimney. His cloth moved in short, forceful circles. Twice the glass rang under his fingers.

"Give me that," she said.

"It's nearly clean."

"Then it should be easy to give me."

He passed her the chimney. A grey crescent remained along its upper rim. Ada wiped it away, using both hands, and set the glass on the folded cloth. Joss unhooked the wick scissors.

"How long?" she asked.

"How long what?"

"Since your hands began to shake."

"They don't shake all day."

"How long?"

He fitted his fingers through the scissor loops. The blades closed a quarter inch above the blackened cloth and opened again.

"Since March," he said. "Worse since August. The right is better in the morning. The left is no use for fine work. I can fill the oil reservoir and wind the lens weight, but I can't always get the char even. An uneven wick smokes, the chimney clouds, and the light loses distance. There. That's the account."

It was six sentences, Ada noted, and more than he had written her on the subject in seven months.

"You should have told me in March."

"You had work."

"I have work now."

"Yes."

The scissors shook between them. Not much. Enough.

Ada held out her hand. "Show me the proper angle."

Joss did not surrender them at once. He looked past her to the sea, where the late light lay flat and metallic out to the horizon. Then he placed the scissors in her palm.

"Not straight across," he said. "A little crown in the middle. Take only the crust. If you cut into the sound cloth, it will burn low until you wind it up."

The first wick took Ada three attempts. She cut too little, and Joss pointed with one stiff finger. On the second she left a black thread at the edge. By the third, the char fell onto the brass tray in one thin piece. The work required less strength than keeping a column of figures aligned, but the same refusal to hurry.

"Again," Joss said.

She trimmed the next. He turned the burner for her, slowly, keeping his hand on the heavier wheel where the tremor mattered less. When the edges were even, she replaced the chimney and fastened its catch.

"Six minutes past lighting time," she said.

"Five by the tower clock."

"The tower clock loses forty seconds a day."

"Thirty-two. I timed it Tuesday."

Ada looked at him. His beard hid most of his mouth, but not the movement at one corner.

"Then we are only three minutes late," she said.

"A scandal."

He touched the taper to the wicks. Flame travelled around the burner, blue at first, then yellow and steady. They lowered the chimney and raised the flame a fraction at a time. Joss watched for smoke. Ada watched his hand on the control. When the lamp burned clean, the lens gathered it and sent its first pale reach over the water.

Below them, the causeway had vanished. Only the marker posts remained, a diminishing row of black strokes between island and mainland. Ada could see the post-house lamp in the town and, after a moment, a smaller green light at the ferry landing. Mara's port lamp, paid for in part by Joss, was exactly where it ought to be.

"You can take the first watch," Joss said. "I'll lie down until ten."

"And after ten?"

"We'll see."

"No. We have seen. I'll take the night."

He began to object, but the left hand had closed over the right again. Ada waited without helping him find a different subject.

"I can't do it alone," he said.

The words were plain and did not improve by repetition. Ada turned to the lamp. One edge of the flame had risen above the others. She adjusted it down until the crown was level.

"Tonight isn't alone," she said.

Joss nodded once. He showed her how far the oil should fall before she filled it, where the spare matches were kept, and which pane of glass rattled in a north wind. Then he went down the turning stair. His steps were careful and evenly spaced. Ada counted twenty-seven before the tower door closed.

Ada set the logbook on a small desk beneath the south window. Its page was ruled into columns for time, wind, weather, visibility, oil used, and remarks. On the shelf below it stood older volumes in a row, their years lettered on the spines in her father's clean hand. Ada counted twenty-three. The earliest covered the year before the *Wren* went down; the next covered the year it did.

She took the pen from its groove. At eight o'clock she entered: Wind west. Weather clear. Visibility eight miles. Light burning properly.

Her figures were smaller than her father's and leaned less. There was room beside them for another hand, but no need for one. Outside, water covered the road in both directions. Inside, the lamp made its measured circle, and each time the light returned it found Ada still at the desk.
