## Round 1 Synthesis (2026-10-01, 6 new sources this round)

### Recurring Patterns

**1. Where there is no central law, hospitality and oaths are the law.** Ithaca without its king is a network of households held together only by xenia, and the suitors' crime is abusing it from the inside (Odyssey). In the Edda, the spoken oath is the only thing holding gods and clans; when it breaks, the result is Ragnarök or the end of a dynasty. In the Mabinogion, sarhaed (honor price) and the rules of the court decide what counts as a crime. In all three, the social contract is not written; it is performed.

**2. Women are the currency of alliance, and the system punishes them when the deal fails.** Branwen is given to join two kingdoms and pays when the alliance breaks; Rhiannon is punished the moment she seems to fail at giving an heir (Mabinogion). Louhi trades her daughters for labor and the Sampo (Kalevala). Manfred treats every woman in his house as a tool for his line (Otranto). Yet the same sources keep showing women as the ones who move the plot: Louhi rules her own land, the women of the Edda keep honor alive through lament and incitement.

**3. Legitimacy comes from outside the center.** The palace in the Turkish tales is static and makes crises; the people who fix them are outsiders (div mothers, the youngest child, women who cross thresholds). In Otranto the legitimate heir is a "peasant". In the Odyssey the king returns as a beggar and is first recognized by a swineherd and an old nurse. The center keeps its power only by taking in agency from the margins.

**4. Endless feud must be stopped from above.** The suitors' families would fight forever if Athena did not impose peace (Odyssey); Edda clans can only end blood feuds through death; Otranto ends with the usurper withdrawing to a convent. Communities in these sources cannot end violence by themselves; they need a higher rule.

### Usable Features for Worldbuilding

- **Guest-right as the only law** (Odyssey, Mabinogion): a small community with no court, where the rules of hosting are the whole legal system. Breaking them is the worst crime.
- **Bride-service instead of bride-price** (Kalevala): the suitor must make something for the bride's family. Marriage as a work contract.
- **Status that rests on reputation, not money** (Turkish tales): the nobles' greatest fear is not poverty but not being able to show their face. Good for a small-town setting.
- **The household in interregnum** (Odyssey): what happens to a family economy when the person who held it together is gone and nobody is sure if they will return.

### Saturated Themes

- Marriage as alliance and exchange is present in five of six sources; more sources from the same tradition add little.
- The usurper vs. the hidden true heir (Otranto, Odyssey, Turkish tales) is well covered.

### Open Areas / Gaps

- No source looks at work and daily economy from the inside: craft, trade and labor appear only as background to nobles.
- Almost every community here is ruled by kings or chiefs; there is no example of a small, flat community (a village, a crew, a guild) solving its own problems.
- Children and old people appear only as helpers or victims, never as social actors.
