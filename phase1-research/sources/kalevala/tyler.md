## Structural Pattern

This text does not move like a single-line "hero's journey" but like runo sequences that fold into each other and each look as if they could be sung on their own. Each large cycle opens with a new beginning: Väinämöinen's birth and sowing; Aino's lament; the origin of iron and the forging of the Sampo; Lemminkäinen's reckless adventures; Kullervo's chain of disasters; the kantele and Sampo cycles; the departure with Marjatta. This cumulative design does not only push events forward; at each return it rebuilds for the listener the characters' voice, epithet and emotional direction.

The study followed five questions: does repetition only support memory, or does it create dramatic time; why do "origin" stories appear at the threshold of a wound or a tool; how is the distance between the one who speaks and the one who acts built; in which scenes does nature stop being only a backdrop; how do names and titles carry a character's function? The text's answer is that repetition works not in front of the event but inside it. Three days, three nights, three tries; six or seven objects; east-west-north-south sequences are not rhythmic filler. They stretch the sense of waiting, searching, mourning or labor in the body. That is why Ilmatar's labor pains, Aino's three days of wandering, the tempering of iron and the kantele playing that spreads over days are performance as much as narrative action.

In scene building, three forms of repetition stand out. The first is **the ladder of failed attempts**: the oak tree, the hostile objects that come out of the furnace before the Sampo, Kullervo who cannot be killed by water/fire/tree, the first kantele that finds its sound only in its maker's hands. The second is **the chain of question and answer**: "Who will carry the news?", "Who will play?", "Where did you come from?", "What shall we do?" The question delays the information; the answer often decides the shape of the next scene. The third is **the alternating repetition of lament**. The words of Aino and Kullervo place the same "it would have been better" pattern inside a different disaster; similar words, instead of giving comfort, announce that fate repeats.

The Sampo cycle gives this scattered structure a strong tie. The Sampo being forged, taken, stolen, falling into the sea and its pieces coming back to shore makes the object not an explained thing but a knot of sound that holds the story together. Thomas A. DuBois's work on the interaction between the oral and the printed Kalevala shows that Lönnrot deliberately expanded and dramatized the forging of the Sampo compared to the oral variants. This means reading the four-stage furnace rhythm in the text not only as a description of craft but as the compiler-author's move to build epic tempo.

## Symbolic / Cosmological Frame (brief, details in Erendiz)

Cosmic forces in the text are less abstract teaching than partners who can speak, be called on and be bargained with. The egg on Ilmatar's knee splitting into sky, earth, light and cloud; iron starting from "milk" and forming a kinship tie; Aino's body and her mother's tears turning into sequences of water, earth, tree and bird make change into a narrative grammar. Nothing disappears: it comes back as a new voice, object or place.

In this frame, prayer is not speech that suspends action but a speech act that is part of the action. Especially in scenes of ships, herds, wounds and birth, the word turns wind, water, tree or heavenly being directly into a partner on stage. The child of Marjatta in the last runo and Väinämöinen's departure do not silence the old order of words at once: the old singer leaves the kantele and his songs behind. So the text voices even the break as a scene of handing over.

## Social Function (brief, details in Wilson)

The social nature of the story shows most clearly in moments of listening and singing together. When the kantele plays, animals, heavenly beings, water folk, old people, children and household workers gather in the same listening community. The wedding runos give separate voices to the bride, the mother, the old woman, the singer and the guest, making the rite of passage many-voiced. This observation is only on the level of form; detailed analysis of power, gender and household order is not the main focus of this file.

## Linguistic Register

The basic voice of the Kalevala is a formula language that keeps changing but is recognized at once. The functional title that follows a character's name keeps the poem's pulse: Väinämöinen "old and steadfast", Ilmarinen "the eternal metal-worker", Lemminkäinen "the reckless", Louhi "hostess" of Pohjola. These epithets give a performative identity instead of psychological depth: the moment the character appears again, the listener hears their sound field, tools and danger. With Kullervo, the titles growing darker and darker make the narrator's judgment heard before the event ends; in contrast, Marjatta's name repeated as "child of beauty" and "virgin mother" builds the sacred, fragile register of the last runo.

Crawford's English translation cannot fully carry the original Finnish sound patterns; so the meter and alliteration visible in translation should not be taken as the same as the system in the source language. Still, short lines of mostly two to four words, parallel syntax and paired adjectives starting with the same consonants leave a trace you can hear in translation. Frog's study of Kalevala meter stresses that the form usually rests on eight-syllable trochaic tetrameter, with alliteration inside the line and semantic/syntactic parallelism as organizing principles. Rakin's study of translations also shows that alliteration is structural in the original but easily becomes incidental in target languages. So here I give weight not to the rhyme the translation seems to have, but to the meaning work done by pairs of lines.

Parallelism is not decoration with synonyms. It grows an image by shifting it slightly at each return: the sea is bed, road and grave; the birch is shelter, stripped body and kantele; iron is brother and also the oath-breaking wounder. This movement keeps abstract feeling inside a material sequence. In Aino's mouth, jewelry, clothes, shore, stone and fish stop being symbols outside the event of the refused marriage offer and become the stages of her escape. After Aino, the scene of choosing a messenger cuts the high register of mourning with the daily habits of animals; when the hare is chosen the tragedy does not get lighter; on the contrary, the narrator shows how small the only "harmless" voice that can carry the pain is.

Scene tension often comes not from the speed of verbs but from the word reaching a threshold. Every time Ilmarinen looks into the furnace, it is broken by the "but" that comes after the object appears; creativity finds the perfect object not at once but from the ashes of rejected forms. In the kantele cycle the same pattern is reversed: everyone plays but no sound comes, and in the end the object calls its own maker. So the authority of art comes not from brute force or seniority but from the closeness between making and performing.

Nature is the text's strongest fellow speaker. Birds look for nests, trees complain, a berry calls out, the sun hides or gives information, a sword reads intent. These personifications are not fairy-tale decoration: even when a character thinks they decide alone, the language of the scene forces them to answer. Kullervo questioning his sword in his last scene is the narrator building his inner judgment outside, as a sharp and irreversible voice. In the Marjatta runo, the two-week-old child addressing Väinämöinen reverses authority with the same method: the smallest voice calls back the singer's past in the form of a formula.

The narrator does not fully stick to the characters. Interventions like "but alas" color in advance Lemminkäinen's recklessness, Kullervo's destructiveness or Aino's fate; yet the right to speak is given to those who suffer, in long monologues. This double layer, a moral frame from outside and dense lament from inside, is the Kalevala's unforgettable technique. The narrator knows the disaster; the character repeats it, names it, spreads it into nature. And in the gap between these two voices, the listener hears both the pattern of mourning and its uniqueness.

### The Wedding, Sound Turning into Ceremony

Runos XX-XXV stretch the single movement of Ilmarinen taking his bride home over six runos, creating a deliberate pause in narrative time. As Venla Sykäri's study of the role of wedding songs in building the *Kalevala* also shows, this is not a feast description added to the plot: the origin story of beer and its three brewing attempts, the invitation, the welcome, the table song, the bride's lament, advice in two directions and the welcome at the second house build the epic center of the rite. The bride's hair, hand and foot being prepared one by one; the threshold, the sledge, the father's house and the new house called up one after another make the passage not a single moment but a threshold stretched out by sound. The beer foams in its vessels and asks for its own praise; the exchange between the child, the old man and Väinämöinen tests who has the right to sing. In the bride's lament, the mother builds the warm past, the old maid the fearful future, the child a cheerful counter-tune; the bride's voice between them turns the parting into a space you can hear through the names of house, threshold, animal, lake, tree and kin. Osmotar's advice to bride and groom slides from formulas of protection to suggestions of violence that are meant to stay hidden; the text does not resolve this contradiction. As Lotte Tarkka's analysis of performance across genres points out, lament, advice, mockery and charm change each other's meaning here.

### Building the Voice after Death

In runos XIV-XV death is sudden while revival is built step by step. Lemminkäinen's confidence, swollen by the tasks of the elk, the horse and the swan, is turned over by his being someone "who does not know the snake's words"; his last words are not a heroic claim but a late need for three words he never learned from his mother. The mother reads the blood on the hairbrush, asks Louhi three times for an answer, then asks the trees, the roads, the Moon and the Sun; each one tells its own trouble and delays the information. With the Sun's sure news the rhythm tightens; then come the search with the rake, finding the limbs, counting the veins, the stitching and the threefold search for honey. The verb of raking, lengthwise, crosswise, in the deep and again, turns the rhythm of mourning into craft labor. The first success is not the end either: the son is alive but blind, deaf and mute. The goal of the bee's three journeys is specifically to bring back the mouth, the song and the charm; the climax is not the body standing up but the first word being heard again.

### The Sound Map of Disease and Healing

Runos XLV-XLIX treat disease, burns and darkness through different registers of address. Counting the nine diseases is an origin story that binds disaster to measure; in Väinämöinen's sauna, stone, water, steam, honey, whisk and salve are named one by one and become healing tools that answer the word. The charm draws a direction from which the disease cannot return, sending it to a series of exile places like the bottom of the sea, a mountain hollow and a bed of iron. Tuukka Karlsson's study, showing that address in Kalevala-meter charms changes depending on whether the addressee is a helper or an enemy, explains the shifts in the text between pleading, bargaining, commanding and driving away. As the fire-child moves layer by layer through the fish, the pattern "one day, the second day, the third day" stretches the pain; Ilmarinen's call to frost, snow and ice builds a long counter-speech against the burning fire. In the Otso cycle, the hunted bear being called "friend", "guest" and "apple of the forest" keeps the tension between act and address: the word does not deny what happened; it places it in another meaning that is ritually bearable.

In this round the primary text was rescanned from start to end; the wedding (XX-XXV), Lemminkäinen's death/revival cycle (XIII-XV) and the disease/healing sequence (XLV-XLIX) were completed with unbroken close reading. Instead of a general summary of style, the analysis follows who the voice passes to in these cycles, how repetition builds the sense of time, and how ritual address becomes stage action.

## Pass 2 Note

The "lost words" in the Antero Vipunen cycle are built not as an abstract store of knowledge but as a residue of sound pulled with effort out of the sleeping singer's body. This detail shows that origin stories do not rule only by naming a thing; the ability to speak is also a material that is bodily, inherited, and stops the story when it runs short. So Väinämöinen's search becomes less the finding of a missing formula than a scene of reconnecting to a broken line of performers.
