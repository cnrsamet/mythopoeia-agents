## Structural Pattern

This book is not a single organic epic but a selection of tales Ignácz Kúnos collected in Anatolia, carried into English by R. Nisbet Bain from the Hungarian edition. The first seventeen tales are under the heading "Turkish Fairy Tales"; the last four, as the preface openly says, are "semi-Turkish" additions of Romanian origin. So the main patterns below are drawn from the Turkish core of the collection; the Romanian additions are kept only to compare the neighboring narrative technique in the same volume.

I ran the reading with five questions: Outside which time do the openings carry the listener? How do the travel sentences manage the pace of the story? What dramatic work do three, forty and forty days/nights do? After a transformation, through which voice or object does the truth return? How much of the voice in the English text belongs to oral performance, and how much to the translator making it literary? The answers meet at the same point: the skeleton of the tale is not only the order of events but a rhythm of listening built through repetition.

Almost every long tale follows a four-beat order: a lack or a taboo; a journey that crosses the threshold; repeated tests; recognition and a closing feast. "The Three Orange-Peris", "The Golden-Haired Children", "The Wind-Demon" and "The World's Most Beauteous Damsel" open this order with desire turning into bodily illness; "The Horse-Devil and the Witch" turns the early breaking of a secret into a debt of travel. The taboo is not only a moral sign: it is the narrator's little latch that gives birth to the second half. If the hero keeps his word the story stays short; if he breaks it, gets curious or chooses wrong, the thresholds multiply.

This multiplying is often in threes: three brothers, three days, three tasks, three hairs, three peris, three transformations. The first two tries teach the pattern of the tension; the third changes the outcome. Forty, on the other hand, is not a short, sharp repetition but a wide frame that makes duration ceremonial: forty peris, forty sons, forty days of prayer, a wedding of forty days and forty nights. Ergüt's analysis of 98 tales in Kúnos's collections using Propp's method shows shared chains of functions under the varied surfaces of events; in this selection too, the chain of taboo/lack, gaining a helper, testing, taking back and recognition is clear.

Helpers are not the scenery of the long road but the story's memory that can be called back. The fish, ant, bird, Anka or horse the hero pities at the start of the road comes back later through a single object or call. This technique turns the first meeting from an "unimportant kindness" into a delayed answer, prepared for the listener without letting them forget the object. On the other side, the witch, the old woman, the jealous servant and the advisers are tied to the same function from the opposite direction: they tighten the knot not through direct force but through wrong interpretation, a substitute bride, the secret of the talisman or curiosity.

The recognition scene is therefore mostly not explanation but performance. In "The Stag-Prince" the siblings' call-and-answer song; in "The Three Orange-Peris" the bird's repeated song; in "Stone-Patience and Knife-Patience" the girl's story that brings the stone to the limit of its patience; in "The Padishah of the Forty Peris" the lament sung to objects make the hidden truth visible through a witness's voice. The ruler is not expected to grasp the truth on his own: the tale makes a song, a ritual, a trace or an object speak what was pushed down.

## Symbolic / Cosmological Frame (brief, details in Erendiz)

The tale universe is layered but not mapped: the seventh world, the seventh sea, the peri garden, under the water, the well and the flight that opens into the sky raise the degree of the threshold more than they draw a direction. Water in particular is a device of transformation and passage: dirty water turns one into a stag, a spring reflects a hidden face, a fish's belly becomes a temporary grave/womb, the water of life joins the bones. Tree, well, garden and palace tie the border to visible objects. Peris and giants are not just poles of good and evil; the "Mother" who helps and the Anka who carries a person are both owners of a dangerous threshold. This flexibility makes the supernatural work as a scene-building force rather than an abstract teaching.

## Social Function (brief, details in Wilson)

Names like padishah, vizier, harem, servant, coffee, pipe, bathhouse and wedding give the story a local surface; but my focus here is not the analysis of power but the order of oral transmission. Even though female characters are often framed as objects of desire or sources of danger, they often start the narrative action: the taboo is broken, the secret is learned, the talisman is asked about, the true bride returns through song. The depictions of Blackness, slavery and ethnicity in the text must be read together with the problematic language of the historical translation; they cannot be taken as a current, neutral picture of Anatolian society.

## Linguistic Register

The most distinctive technique is the "impossible past" opening. Straw being a sieve, animals being craftsmen, kinship ties turned upside down, or the narrator walking and riding through the air at the same time suspend the contract of logic in the very first sentence. These rhymes give no information about the event; they open the threshold between narrator and listener. Karagöz's definition of formulas as special sayings with duties at the start of the story, in its transitions and at its end explains how they work in this volume: the formula is not decoration but a tool of rhythm, attention and taking the floor. The travel sentences do the same job: "he went and he went", pairs of mountain/valley, long/short road manage the narrator's breath and the change of scene more than they measure physical distance.

This rhythm draws strength from paired and threefold parallels: "open your eyes / close your eyes", "sweep and do not sweep", "right/left", "day/night", "up/down". Riddle-like commands, rather than forcing the character to reason, keep the listener busy on the level of sound and delay the solution. "The Piece of Liver" shows this principle in its barest form: the chain sentence grows longer with each new link, then undoes the same chain in reverse. Syntax is not the carrier of the event but the very machine of the event.

The narrator is very visible. Interventions like "believe it or not", "what do you say?", "I forgot the best part" give the impression of a performer testing the listener's attention rather than a distant all-knowing author. In danger the sentences get shorter and the sounds multiply: cracking, humming, wings beating, an animal's call, a stone's hiss. At the feast the sentences widen by piling up objects, colors, food and music. Tension is announced through cutting and broken sound; reward through listing and excess.

But the language at hand is not a transparent record of this oral voice. The preface openly accepts the line from Turkish oral storytelling to Hungarian, and from there to Bain's English. Türkmen's study of translation and ideology in the Kúnos tales also draws attention to the rewriting effect of word choice and cultural transfer. So the archaic forms of address, the heavy "Oriental" decoration and some racializing adjectives in the English text should be read not as proof of Turkish performance but as the voice of the transmission layer. In contrast, the architecture of repetition, the order of numbers, the calls and answers and the closing prayers stay visible as the carrying skeleton even after translation.

The closings are the reverse movement of the openings. The opening turns the world upside down and takes the listener out of everyday time; the last sentence brings them back with a feast, a good wish or the comic crumb that falls to the narrator's share. "Forty days and forty nights" is not only an announcement of happiness: it is the final measure that ends the repetitions of tension. The most productive narrative lesson to take from this volume is not the motifs themselves but how the motifs are managed through sound: every miracle comes with a repetition, every loss with a trace, every recognition with a song or an object.

## Pass 2 Note

The Div Mother and similar female figures show that formulaic language is not only a contract between narrator and listener: kinship forms of address like "little mother" and "sister" are speech acts that actually open the passage inside the scene. So the hero enters the dangerous place not only with courage but with the right pitch of voice and the right name for the relationship; the form of address becomes the invisible talisman of the journey. This ties to the same narrative principle as the witness role the tale gives to song and object at moments of recognition: here the word does not only describe, it changes the threshold.
