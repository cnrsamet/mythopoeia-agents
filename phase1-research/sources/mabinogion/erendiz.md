## Structural Pattern

The collection's twelve tales come from two different layers: the four "Branches of the Mabinogi" (Pwyll, Branwen, Manawyddan, Math) carry a core Brythonic cosmology, while the remaining texts (Owain/The Lady of the Fountain, Peredur, Geraint, Culhwch and Olwen, The Dream of Rhonabwy, The Dream of Macsen, Lludd and Llefelys, Taliesin) either dilute this core by tying it to Arthur's court or keep it as a separate piece of cosmogony. For someone moving through the whole text in one reading, the most striking repeating skeleton is less the plot than an architecture of thresholds: the door (the third door at Gwales), the passage/shallow water (Bran becoming a bridge between Wales and Ireland), the yard stone/horse block (Rhiannon's punishment), the edge of the forest (Glyn Cuch, Pwyll's point of entry into Annwn), the exact center of the island (where Lludd buries the dragons), and the mouth of the cauldron (Bran's cauldron of rebirth, Ceridwen's cauldron of inspiration). The story is always built on a border being crossed rightly or wrongly; the hero's virtue is almost never measured by a moral choice but by whether he follows the protocol of a threshold (the rule of hospitality, the rule of the single blow, the question that must or must not be asked).

A second pattern is number itself being used as a cosmological device: "the three unfortunate blows", "three good/bad disclosures", Ysbaddaden's forty anoethau (impossible tasks), healing/transformation repeated three times (Gilfaethwy and Gwydion punished for three years as deer, boar and wolf), the eagle in the tree brought back down to human shape by an englyn sung three times. The triad here is not decorative style but an operational grammar that shows how the world is "locked" and how it is "opened".

## Symbolic / Cosmological Frame

**Annwn: a horizontal, not vertical, otherworld.** Pwyll's meeting with Arawn sets the metaphysical ground of this collection: Annwn is neither hell nor heaven, only a neighboring sovereignty; its rules (killing with a single blow, refusing the second blow), its hierarchy (a crowned king), its hunting culture and its court are exactly the same as this world's. Arawn "dressing" Pwyll in his own shape, and this lasting a year without any corruption (Pwyll does not touch Arawn's wife), sets out a very clear principle: the difference between the sacred (the otherworld king) and the profane (the mortal prince) is not essential but functional; one can perfectly fill the other's place, because the distinction is a matter of status, not of being. This is a sharp break from classic vertical cosmologies (underworld/sky, dead/living): entering Annwn is no more than crossing the edge of a forest, the way back is always open, and the only "sin" is crossing this border rudely (Pwyll setting his own hounds on the other's prey).

**Sovereignty is punished through the female body, not the land.** Rhiannon is tested twice: first through her horse's "slow but unreachable" pace that no rider can catch (this codes her as a horse goddess/sovereignty figure; the academic literature links her to the Gaulish Epona), then by being accused of the lie that she "ate" her son and being punished. The form of the punishment is not random: sitting by the horse block and offering to carry guests on her back, that is, performing a crude, animalized parody of her own horse nature, and at the threshold of the gate, at a point neither inside nor outside. The punishment of the sovereignty figure is always built as reducing her body to its border function (a body defined by waiting at the threshold, carrying, the capacity to give birth/continue the line); in the Manawyddan branch the same logic repeats: the punishment of Rhiannon and Pryderi is carrying donkey collars and door knockers around their necks, again a punishment tied to threshold objects (door, carrying).

**Incomplete resurrection: speech as the price of death.** Bran's cauldron brings the dead back but does not give back the power of speech. This single detail is the collection's densest metaphysical claim: there is no real defeat of death here, only a silent repetition. Logos (the word) is not the free part of existence, it is exactly what death takes away; the cauldron gives back the body but cannot give back the person/the story. Efnisien throwing himself into the cauldron and breaking both the cauldron and his own heart is the only real death that steps out of this cycle; it shows that what is "irreversible" within the cosmos can only be brought about by a conscious sacrifice turned on oneself. Bran's cut-off head staying both alive and free of pain at Gwales for eighty years as long as "the third door" stays closed, and then all the grief and history coming back at once when the door is opened, is another version of the same principle: time and grief can be suspended as long as a threshold taboo is kept; history itself flows back through a border breach.

**Deferring death as an engineering problem.** Ysbaddaden Chief Giant, knowing he will die when his daughter marries, builds an endlessly receding chain of anoethau that ties each task to an impossible precondition of the one before ("even if you get this, there is this you will not get..."). This is a narrative engine for delaying death: the old sovereignty (the giant), knowing it cannot stop the arrival of the new order (the groom, the new generation), tries to stretch not time but the chain of causes. At the end of the chain, every link is completed by Arthur's collective strength. So the text claims that no delaying mechanism, however layered, can keep a decided death/succession on hold forever. Lleu Llaw Gyffes's condition of death ("neither in a house nor outside, neither on horseback nor on foot, neither clothed nor naked") repeats the same logic on an individual scale: the hero's death can only be made possible by building an impossible in-between state (one foot on the edge of a bath, one foot on a goat's back). So here too death cannot happen unless it is squeezed outside ordinary time and space, into an exact threshold. And even when it happens it is not "death" but turning into an eagle with rotting flesh, that is, moving into another liminal state; he is brought down from the tree and turned back into human shape by Gwydion's three englynion (naming repeated three times). Naming/repetition is the only ritual technology here that reverses death.

**Shape-shifting as the price of knowledge.** Gwion Bach's escape from Ceridwen (hare-greyhound, salmon-otter, bird-hawk, grain of wheat-hen) is a classic chain of elements (land, water, air, back to earth/grain), but its end is not a typical "capture" but a swallowing-pregnancy-rebirth: Ceridwen swallows him as a grain of wheat, carries him nine months, throws him into the sea. Knowledge (awen/inspiration) here is not a gift given willingly but something stolen by crossing a border without permission (the mouth of the cauldron, the three drops that jump onto his finger), then "cleansed" by passing through a full cycle of death-womb-water. Taliesin's own identity poems ("I was in the ark with Noah", "I was there in the time of Abel and Cain", "it is not known whether my body is flesh or fish") widen this principle into a cosmogony: the poet is built as someone who has held all historical/cosmic time through endless shape-shifting. Knowledge comes not from a fixed identity but from keeping identity in a constant threshold state.

**The question not asked: the collection's one wound that does not close.** The bleeding spear and the cut-off head carried on a platter that Peredur sees at the court of the wounded king make up the only scene the text itself openly interprets: if the question had been asked the king would have healed and his lands found peace; since it was not asked, the wars and widowhoods will go on. While in the rest of the collection the cosmic order is always repaired by an act (a single blow, the right word, the right horse, the right magic), here the repair mechanism is only an epistemic act, asking, and this act fails. Peredur later kills the witches of Gloucester (interests tied to his own family), but the question of why the spear bleeds is never answered in the text. This is the kind of inconsistency the role definition looks for: against the confident, closing threshold logic of the rest of the collection, here is the only metaphysical tension that is truly unhealed, truly left open.

If we bring all these threads together: the Mabinogion's basic existential question is not "how did the world move from chaos to order" but "how is order kept as long as the thresholds are worked correctly". The sacred/profane line is a matter of protocol, not identity (Annwn); sovereignty is bound to the body, especially the female body and its threshold function (Rhiannon); death is never fully defeated, only delayed or reduced to silence/liminality (Bran, Lleu); and knowledge, just like death, is won by crossing a border without permission (Gwion Bach). Even the three plagues in Lludd and Llefelys confirm the same law: each is a border breach (words carried by the wind, a scream that reaches every hearth, stolen provisions crossing the border of the store), and each is solved only by finding an absolute border point like the "exact center" of the island.

## Social Function (brief, details in Wilson)

The duty of hospitality, the law of the duel settled by a single blow, alliance and transfer of land through marriage (Branwen, Olwen), and how the forty anoethau are bound up with a feudal/clan economy of honor all give rich material on how power relations work in practice, but going deeper is Wilson's area.

## Linguistic Register (brief, details in Tyler)

Formal patterns stand out, such as set greetings ("Heaven prosper thee"), the huge catalogue of lineage/praise in the Culhwch tale, Taliesin's anaphoric "I was..." cosmogony poems and the threefold duel of praise and insult (the bards' verbal duel), but the analysis of their style/rhythm is Tyler's job.

## Pass 2 Note

Wilson's reading of Blodeuwedd as a "wife" made by magic only for Lleu, without a natural network of kin, brings out a category I skipped in my own frame: Blodeuwedd is the only figure in the collection who is made to exist without birth, that is, without passing through any threshold. She is a body built directly from flowers, with no womb and no border breach. That is why her punishment is structurally different from the other shape-shiftings (Gwion Bach, Gilfaethwy, Lleu): theirs are temporary and reversible, while the owl form is permanent and exiles her from the other birds forever (unable to be seen by day, the hostility of the other birds). Since she never crossed the threshold of birth, her punishment is to be frozen forever at a threshold (neither a bird of night nor of day); this is the most radical and irreversible version of the collection's law that "if a threshold is not respected, the punishment is locked into the body".
