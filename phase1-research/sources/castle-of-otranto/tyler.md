## Structural Pattern

On its very first page, the novel places one more narrator inside the story: the preface of the "translator" who supposedly carries an Italian manuscript into English makes the text both old and doubtful. This outer frame is not only historical decoration. The reader is prepared to meet the miraculous events not directly as Walpole's invention but as a legend inherited from an old text. The fake origin E. J. Clery draws attention to, and the "Gothic" label in the second edition, show that the work's novelty of genre came from the start with a game of masks. So the novel stages not so much a wish to be believed as how the act of believing is built.

The main story flows in five short chapters, like an almost unbroken three-day crisis. Each chapter knots on a missing piece of information, a wrong inference or a new sign; the next chapter opens it only partly. Conrad's death under the giant helmet, the ancestor stepping out of the portrait, the escape through the vaults, Frederic's armored arrival, the lines of the prophecy and Alfonso's apparition growing to giant size at the end are separate links of the same chain. The prophecy appears first as an obscure sentence, then as pieces of armor, and finally as a bodily ghost. This order does not put explanation before the event; it first makes the reader crash into the image and delays the meaning.

The novel's basic dramatic movement was tested with these questions: How is terror turned into delay? Why does the castle become not a background but the driver of the story? What changes when the giant objects appear piece by piece? How do voices set the characters apart? What kind of reading attitude does the fake translator in the preface produce? The answers come together in one order: in Otranto, knowledge only comes after doors, portraits, stairs and words heard wrong. The reader, like Isabella, thinks they have found a passage; then the ground changes the direction of the story.

In the preface Walpole describes "terror" as the main tool and "pity" as its counter-beat; the text applies this program not mechanically but like a pulse. Right after Manfred's command and threat comes Isabella's panic; right after the image of the giant armor comes Bianca's rambling chatter; before Matilda's death comes the language of forgiveness. That recent assessments of Gothic drama read the work as a kind of stage direction is therefore apt: entrances, closed doors, sudden sounds, objects carried on stage and news learned in the wings manage not the plot of the novel but its tempo.

## Symbolic / Cosmological Frame (brief, details in Erendiz)

The helmet, the sword, the hand, the foot and finally the whole ghost go into circulation like missing parts of a single giant body. This piecemeal appearance turns the prophecy of "the real owner" growing too large for the castle from a visual riddle into a physical certainty. The scale of the objects goes beyond the human; yet the text's effect comes less from building an abstract teaching than from ordinary things suddenly changing scale and losing their reliability.

St. Nicholas, Alfonso's tomb, dreams and the bleeding statue form the text's repertoire of signs. Still, the sacred is not a calming explanation in the story: the signs often appear at the same moment as a character's desire or fear, and delay the meaning. The final apparition joins the earlier scattered images in hindsight; so the miracle, besides being a surprise, is a last montage that gathers the story's scattered marks.

## Social Function (brief, details in Wilson)

The text keeps repeating the language of titles and kinship: "Prince", "Marquis", "father", "daughter", "heir". But on the narrative level these names become not fixed identities but roles that break easily. Theodore is named first "peasant", then the lost son, finally the heir; Manfred swings between "Prince" and "tyrant/usurper". These changes of name make the revealing of lineage a knot of the story.

The contrast between the female characters' language of obedience and piety and Manfred's language of command is especially audible. Hippolita's constantly softening sentences, Matilda's withdrawing forms of address and Isabella's increasingly sharp refusal make authority heard in three different tones in the same scene. This observation is less about the text's social judgment than about how power is felt in the rhythm of speech.

## Linguistic Register

The narrator's third-person voice does not stay inside the characters for long; it gives a bodily sign, a short hesitation or a sudden thought, and returns at once to action. Verbs of movement like "saw", "stopped", "listened", "trembled", "ran" narrow the sentences, especially in Isabella's escape. The telling in the underground passage lines up the creak of a door, wind, a lamp going out, moonlight and footsteps heard; the information about cause and effect is reduced while the sensory signs multiply. Fear here, before any description of a monster, is the senses working incompletely.

This technique matches Cynthia Wall's reading that the text's syntactic passages work like underground passages. Although the dialogue marks in the Otranto edition are clear to the modern reader, the frequent and sudden handover of speech blurs the character for a moment. Especially in the Bianca-Jaquez-Diego scenes, interruptions, repetitions and the feeling of "who is speaking?" multiply the fear itself. Describing corridors by doors on the right/left is not only a spatial scheme either: the sentence forces the reader to choose a direction, then voids the choice with a new voice or object.

Manfred's voice is built from short, commanding and clipped questions. He keeps asking "where?", "who?", "speak!"; his search for information turns talk from dialogue into interrogation. But this certainty cracks before the supernatural signs: broken exclamations, half-finished confessions and questions aimed at himself take the place of commands. Against this, Theodore's language is made of formally long declarations carrying moral certainty; Matilda's and Hippolita's language moves through pleading, forgiveness and indirect speech. So the characters are told apart not through psychological inner analysis but through the pressure and length of their sentences.

Bianca is the text's most useful counter-voice. Her speech delays the information: before telling the truly terrible thing she talks about the ring, the stairs, her own hair, an old suitor; Manfred's haste and her straying into side paths stretch the scene tight. This comic register does not scatter the terror. On the contrary, it keeps the object of fear invisible for a few more sentences; then the giant hand comes out harder from inside this chatter that interrupts the explanation. Just as decisive as Walpole setting terror against pity is his giving terror rhythm through comic talk.

Naming completes this order of voices. Alfonso settles in with the fixed epithet "the Good", St. Nicholas with a repeated holy name, and Manfred through the tension between his official title and his moral description. Italian place and personal names and high English rhetoric create a deliberately artificial tone, fitting the fake-translation frame of the text. Ian Duncan's observation about Walpole's experiment in joining old and modern forms of romance becomes concrete here: the old is heard through the ceremonial language of prophecy and titles; the modern through fast scene changes, the characters' momentary mistakes and the noise of everyday talk.

The most memorable technique is the giant object becoming first an image, then a sound, then a key to the story. The helmet crushes Conrad; its plumes wave at the level of the windows; the foot of the armor appears in a closed room; the hand appears at the top of the stairs; in the end the whole body speaks. In this rising series the object stops being decoration and gains a verb: it gives news, blocks, frightens, ties events together. Otranto's staying power lies less in not explaining the supernatural than in spreading it into the rhythm of place and language.

## Pass 2 Note

The line Erendiz draws between false divine discourse and true supernatural causality can also be read in how the voices are spread: Manfred speaks his own desire in commanding formulas like "the will of heaven"; but as the crisis grows, these words dissolve into broken exclamations and unanswered questions. In contrast, the supernatural first stays silent as object and sound, and only at the end builds the single, clear sentence that marks Theodore. So the novel's highest voice of authority is given not to the character who talks the most but to the stage machinery that long delays speaking.
