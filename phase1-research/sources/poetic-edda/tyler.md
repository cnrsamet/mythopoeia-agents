## Structural Pattern

This collection is not a single epic in the modern sense but a series of dense scenes built for a listener who already knows a shared stock of stories. In the mythological part, the poetry picks the knot instead of telling all the events: *Völuspá* squeezes the past and the coming ruin into the seeress's visions; *Vafþrúðnismál* turns knowledge into a deadly question-and-answer contest; *Grímnismál* dramatizes a catalogue of names and places through the unveiling of a hidden identity. Catalogue, prophecy and dialogue are therefore tools that put pressure on the pace of the story in different ways.

Short four-line units each give one piece of information, one image or one counter-move. In *Völuspá*, the return of "would you know yet more?" builds the seeress's superiority at regular intervals. In *Vafþrúðnismál* the counted questions and answers do the same job; the last question having no answer creates a climax without action. In *Grímnismál* the series of names produce dramatic irony, as the listener grasps Othin's identity before the king does.

Hávamál has an additive structure: proverbs, the repeated formula addressed to Loddfáfnir, the story of the mead of poetry, the winning of the runes and the magic songs are joined not by one event but around "the gaining and use of speech". This looseness is not a flaw but a carrier suited to adding pieces in performance and keeping them in memory. Two separate academic studies of performance that I scanned also treat Eddic poetry as performance that lived in oral circulation before being written down; so the repetitions here are not decoration but tools of composition and of guiding the listener.

The prose frames in Bellows's edition are also decisive. The verse often does not give the summary of the event; the prose fixes where the action happens, while the lines are saved for the tensest moment of speech, vision and threat. The lesson for worldbuilding: keep explanation in the skeleton and make the poetic text dense only at the moment of decision, conflict or vision.
The heroic poems sharpen this principle further: they are not "life stories" but moments of decision cut from a cycle the listener already knows. *Völundarkviða* leaves all the in-between steps of abandonment, maiming and revenge to the prose links and concentrates the confrontation in the speeches of Völund, Nithuth and Böðvild. In the Helgi poems, the tactics of battle are hardly told; a verbal duel, the image of ships, a meeting among the dead or a single night's visit to the grave mound carry the emotional result of the war. *Atlakviða* takes this cutting and squeezing to the extreme; *Atlamál* widens the same skeleton with women reading runes and telling dreams. So the difference is not only length but an ethics of staging: one brings forward the bones of the event, the other the waiting at home and the late warnings.

This fragmented form is not a lack but the source of tension. Lost, contradictory or misplaced stanzas are real in the manuscript history of the text, but the poems' effect often comes from this omission: who killed, whose word is true, which oath came first are questions no narrator closes. The heroic world is built not like an exact chronicle but like an echo chamber where different mouths circle the same wound.

## Symbolic / Cosmological Frame (brief, details in Erendiz)

The cosmic order is heard not as an abstract scheme but through concrete objects that can recur: the creature gnawing Yggdrasil's root, the horn, the chain, fire, the sea. *Völuspá* grows the ruin through visual and sound signs: the tree shakes, roosters crow, the hound howls, the sun goes dark. The coming of the new world is given not through explanation but through counter-images such as green earth and fields bearing crops on their own. Cosmology thus works as a stage set that keeps widening.
In the heroic part, this frame becomes narrower, more bodily and spatial: a grave mound opening, a ring of fire, a snake pit, a horse bowing its head, a bed soaked in blood, the sound of a harp coming from a far road. These repeat not to explain the universe but to make the point of no return visible. In *Helgakviða Hundingsbana II* especially, the warm body of Sigrún entering the grave mound and Helgi's cold, wet deadness are made to collide in the same scene; abstract mourning gives way to a climate you can touch.

## Social Function (brief, details in Wilson)

Hávamál's rulings on hospitality, measure, gifts, friendship and speech tie a rule of conduct to a saying that can be memorized. What matters from Tyler's view is less the norm than the sound of the norm: the return of the same syntactic openings turns advice from a personal view into a speech act that can be passed on. In the knowledge contests too, the effect of knowing shows less in direct teaching than in seizing the turn to speak and the last word.
In the heroic poems, women's speech takes on this function in another form: warning, lament, curse, prophecy and witness. Kostbera reads the runes, Glaumvör tries to interpret dreams; the men dismiss them, and the poem keeps the coming disaster waiting in their unheard words. The laments of Sigrún and Guðrún also rebuild the reputation of the dead. The key narrative function here is turning the silence left after war into a single, bodily, audible voice.

## Linguistic Register

The main movement of Eddic language is compression. Bellows's English translation does not carry the sound of the original Old Norse one to one; but the pause between two half-lines that the translator keeps, the stressed words and the alliteration make visible that the poetry thinks in short units. A line often first strikes the object or name, and in the second half ties it to a verb, a threat or a fate. Chosen compounds and titles do the work instead of a mass of adjectives.

In Othin's many names this technique becomes a narrative machine. The one who enters as Gagnráth in *Vafþrúðnismál* and as Grímnir in *Grímnismál* is not just explained by the names listed later; each name widens his reach and opens the hidden identity in a rhythmic climb. So the name lists are not just data dumps: sound likeness and sequence produce a sense of power in the listener.

Voice and distance shift too. In *Völuspá* the seeress is both the witness of the vision and the owner of the word; repetitions of the "I saw / I know" type put her authority ahead of the events. In Hávamál the commanding voice is shadowed by the price and trickery in winning the mead of poetry. In *Vafþrúðnismál* the narrator steps back; the pattern of questions and the choice of the last riddle reveal the character in action.

The images make abstract feeling bodily and audible: the bird of forgetfulness, the road and the threshold of the house; howling, horn, trembling, blood and flame; burning between two fires, a falling sword, a hall built of weapons. In creative transfer, the most useful technique of this source is to build the scene with a threshold, a tool, an animal's sound, a movement of the body and a recurring object instead of naming "fear" or "fate".

When all the heroic poems are tested through this lens, the linguistic register does not stay in one "epic" tone. *Helgakviða Hundingsbana I* flows like a fairly sequential war ballad; the second Helgi poem is pieces of speech pinned together with prose. This pinning is not weakness but a technique of performance: the prose lets the listener in through the door, and the verse only holds the moment when the voice becomes irreversible. The broken quality of *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu* and *Hamðismál* works for the same reason: not the whole past remains, only the last few words where the oath was broken or the wrong decision could not be taken back.

### Sub-questions for This Round

1. **How do the heroic poems make violence felt without telling it?** Often they give the object of the result instead of the battle: Högni's heart that does not tremble, Gunnar's harp, the skulls Völund turns to silver, the bloody bed and the bodies to be stoned. The action stays closed; the object becomes both proof and echo.
2. **Does dialogue take the place of decision?** Yes. *Grípisspá* lets the future be lived in advance through questions and answers; the short words of Brynhild, Gunnar and Högni seem to have already done the killing during the argument. In *Helreið Brynhildar* the journey becomes a conversation of accusation and defense with the giantess. The dramatic dialogue and economy of implication stressed by secondary studies of style are clearly visible in this setup in the primary text: the characters do not explain their intentions; they press on the missing information in each other's words.
3. **What job does the verbal duel do?** The exchange between Sinfjötli and Guthmund is a spoken miniature of the physical fight before battle. Insults and charges about lineage and gender occupy the field of speech more than they test the other side's courage. Helgi's warning, "get back to the fight", matters: the poem enjoys the rhythm of the exchange but does not put it in place of heroism; the word rehearses for the sword.
4. **How is the female voice built in lament?** *Guðrúnarkviða I* starts not with tears but with tears not coming. Other people's stories of loss do not help; when Gollrönd uncovers the body and lays Sigurðr's head on Guðrún's knee, the numbness breaks. Hair, a warm cheek, tears falling like rain and the sound of geese in the yard carry the inner pain out without explanation. Brynhild's single laugh that rings through the building is a counter-rhythm to this weeping: two women make rival sound records of the same death.
5. **Does lament only mourn?** No. *Guðrúnarkviða II* rebuilds a life story inside the voice by bringing, one after another in the first person, the past, the horse's grief, the war picture in the weaving, the ingredients of the drink of forgetting and Atli's dreams. *Oddrúnargrátr* puts birth magic at the threshold of lament: Oddrún first eases another woman's labor pains with words and hands, then tells her own loss. Healing speech and mourning speech become two modes of the same voice.

The women's voices of these poems are not one ideal type. Böðvild appears only in the last, limited speech where she can say she was forced; Sigrún calls the dead and curses; Brynhild builds her own life story against the accusation of a rival narrator; Guðrún is at times a frozen witness, at times a sharp speaker, at times the mother who starts revenge; Oddrún carries the passage between the one who helps and the one who mourns. This variety tells the heroic cycle not only in the voice of the male warrior but also through the emptiness the voice leaves after war.

Repetition here does less to restate the same event than to move the subject. The formula "she did not weep" prepares, across Guðrún's different laments, a voice that is first held back and then bursts; the same image of the bed returns as the place where Sigurðr was killed, the place of Brynhild's parting and Helgi's short union in the grave. Concrete words like heart, hand, hair, horse, ring, sword, door and threshold pass into the hands of another actor at each return. The usable result for worldbuilding: instead of fixing a symbol with a dictionary definition, hand it over in the next scene to another character and another feeling.

The stressed structure of Bellows's translation, based on two half-lines, does not fully represent the sound of Old Norse; so the basis here is not the sound value of the original language but the scene economy, formulaic repetition and order of speech that can survive translation. Still, as the secondary academic readings point out, the strength of Eddic poetry lies in an economy that is not the same as plainness, and in dramatic dialogue that tests the characters' choices in their own mouths. This is confirmed consistently in the women's laments, prophecy speeches and verbal duels of the primary text.

## Pass 2 Note

Erendiz treating oath-breaking as the axis that joins cosmic and human ruin, and Wilson reading it as the base of spoken law, sharpen a formal detail for me: in the Edda, order shows first not through the narrator's judgment but through spoken words being repeated, left unfinished and tested by counter-speech. So the breaks, verbal duels and laments in the heroic poems do not only intensify feeling; inside the scene, they make the listener weigh which word is binding and which came too late.
