## Structural Pattern
Völuspá (the Prophecy of the Seeress), the first and most defining poem of "The Poetic Edda", has a cyclical narrative skeleton that runs from the creation of the world to its destruction (Ragnarök) and then its rebirth. The story is framed as Othin (Odin), the highest god, raising a dead seeress (völva) to get knowledge from her. This frame creates a hierarchical tension by placing the source of knowledge outside the divine center, in a marginalized or old (dead/female) figure. The other mythological poems of the collection (Hávamál, Thrymskvitha and so on) are built as episodic social manuals that test worldly codes of social behavior, rules for survival and how permeable social borders (gender, status) are, through stories among the gods.

## Symbolic / Cosmological Frame (brief, details in Erendiz)
The text draws a nine-world model of the universe shaped around the tree Yggdrasil. Creation being shaped from the body of the giant Ymir, and everything being destroyed in fire/water at Ragnarök, stress the cyclical nature of nature and life. The cosmic order is subject to the inevitability of fate (the Norns).

## Social Function
This text works as a social contract that justifies Scandinavian society's own internal conflicts, processes of integration and cultural changes behind a mythological curtain.

First, **the war of the Aesir and the Vanir** is a symbolic myth of social union. The war is between the Aesir, who stand for war, rule and hierarchy, and the Vanir, who stand for fertility, farming and the forces of nature (and also the transgressive magic called *seiðr*). In a structural-functionalist reading, this conflict and the peace that follows justify the integration of the warrior/aristocratic class and the farming/producing class in Scandinavian society. The two groups giving each other hostages (Njord, Freyr, Freya) and creating Kvasir, the god of wisdom, by spitting into a shared vessel show that social stability is only possible when different classes reconcile and meet on the ground of a shared law (contract).

Second, **the authority of the Völva figure** stands out. That even Odin, the greatest god, must turn to a woman, a seeress (Völva), to learn the future reflects the alternative center of power that prophecy, magic and ancient knowledge held over patriarchal warrior authority in Scandinavian society. It is an admission that rational/military leadership (Odin) depends on natural or fatalistic forces it cannot control (the Norns, the Völva).

Third, **the Ragnarök story** reflects a social crisis and change. This apocalypse, in which even the gods are dragged into unavoidable destruction, may express the collective anxiety of pre-Christian Scandinavian society about the collapse of its own old order. The fall of the old figures of authority, followed by the founding of a cleaner, new world, is an ideology of "rebirth and comfort" offered to the people in times of social ruin. By stressing that even the gods lose their legitimacy when they betray the law and oaths they set up, the text urges absolute compliance with social contracts and loyalty (the legal order).

Fourth, **the practical ethics of life in Hávamál ("Sayings of the High One")** is the codification of the insecure, high-risk and reputation-centered structure of Viking Age society. This wisdom, passed on in Odin's voice (rules of hospitality, keeping friendships, moderation and the need to be on guard at all times), is a survival guide for a society where power and status are protected not only by military strength but by social networks. The belief that all that remains of a person's earthly life is "an honorable name" is the only anthropological mechanism that secures self-control, courage and social order in these communities where central authority is weak.

Fifth, **Thrymskvitha and the Crossing of Social Borders** shows how the strict gender roles of Norse culture (*ergi*, the taboo on effeminacy) can be stretched in moments of social crisis. Thor, the most "masculine" god, dressing as a woman (a bride) to get back from the giants his hammer (Mjölnir), the guarantee of order, is a temporary deconstruction of extreme masculinity through comedy. Loki running the operation in the role of a "maid" shows the trickster figure's function in crossing social borders. The story reflects that when the survival of the group (saving the hammer) is at stake, even gender transgressions that are normally shameful can be tolerated and justified.

Sixth, **the Heroic Poems (the Sigurth and Helgi cycles) and Honor Culture** are a sociological tool that justifies Norse aristocratic values and the institution of the blood feud. In societies without a central state or justice system, the blood feud is not mere chaos of violence but an institutional duty that secures social order, balance of power and justice. Not answering a murder or betrayal means the "social death" of the clan. Helgi's wars and the spiraling webs of betrayal in the Sigurth cycle (Brynhild, Gudrun, Gunnar) idealize a "stoic warrior" model where protecting one's honor is put above even one's life.

Seventh, **the Role of Women in Blood Feud and Conflicts of Loyalty** plays a critical part in these heroic poems. Even though noble women like Gudrun and Brynhild do not fight physically, they are moral authorities who incite men to revenge and keep social memory/honor alive. These poems stage the deadly tension between clan loyalty based on blood and alliances made by oath. That the heroes do not step back even though they foresee their own ruin (fate/wyrd) is an ideology that dictates social duty above individual survival.

## Linguistic Register (brief, details in Tyler)
The text is passed on in a poetic and archaic language. The seeress's refrain, "Would you know yet more?", creates a ritualistic, hypnotic effect. Hávamál uses a didactic and gnomic style that makes sure vital social rules are memorized and passed from generation to generation.

## Pass 2 Note
Erendiz naming "oath-breaking" as the only real category of sin on both the cosmic and the human level proves that in this society without a central state, the spoken contract (the oath) is the law itself; the collapse of the oath (Ragnarök or Atlakvitha) is directly the same as social collapse. Also, Tyler pointing out that the verbal duels before battle and the women's voices in the laments are acts that decide reputation makes clear that "honor" (reputation) is a social capital built and defended not only by physical strength but in the field of language/speech.
