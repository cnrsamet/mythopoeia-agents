# Style Contract: Saltreach

> Locked for Phase 3. Changes only with the user's approval.

This file is the voice of one world. Another world gets its own contract.
The seven base rules at the end are shared by every world.

## Creative Identity

The narrator of Saltreach is a patient witness who trusts small, exact
things more than big words. The island is told through what people
handle every day: the tide table pinned by the door, the wick scissors,
the logbook's ruled columns, the ferry's tarred rope, a kettle that
takes too long on a damp morning. Large feelings are never announced.
They show in how a person holds a cup, which chair they choose, what
they write down and what they leave out.

The tone is quiet but not cold. Grief is real here and so is ordinary
life around it: gossip at the post, bad jokes about the weather, the
satisfaction of a lamp that burns clean. The story does not punish its
people for their past; it asks them to look at it.

## Narrative Tense and Rhythm

The main narration is in the simple past. Memories of the night of the
Wren are told in the past perfect only for their first sentence or two,
then slip into the simple past, so the reader stands inside them rather
than outside.

Rhythm follows the tide. Scenes of work are calm and ordered: one task
after another, in the order a keeper would do them. Scenes of
confrontation slow down rather than speed up; people pause, look at
something else, start a sentence again.

A paragraph is a unit of thought, not of breath. Short sentences can
live inside long paragraphs. Do not give every line of dialogue or every
small action its own paragraph.

## Voice and Point of View

Close third person, held on Ada for the whole story. The narrator never
knows what Ada cannot know. Ada is a bookkeeper, so she notices numbers,
columns, totals and errors before she notices faces; her mind reaches
for the ledger when she is afraid.

Joss and Mara are seen only from outside, through what they say, do and
avoid. Their inner life is shown through objects and habits, never
summarized.

## Dialogue

People on Saltreach speak plainly and a little sideways. They talk about
the weather, the tide and the lamp when they mean something else, but at
least once in each scene someone says the real thing directly.

Joss speaks in short, practical sentences and changes the subject to
work. Mara is loud, quick and the only one who swears. Ada speaks
carefully and asks precise questions.

## Base Rules (shared by every world)

- Dialogue is written with quotation marks, not dashes.
- Dialogue length follows the needs of the scene; when needed it becomes a
  real, long conversation. Back-to-back lines are not left bare; action,
  a look or a reaction comes between them. In every chapter, at least
  once, a character speaks without interruption for at least 4-5
  sentences: tells a memory, explains a feeling, defends something.
- Warmth is required: every chapter has at least one moment of real relief,
  joking or friendship. It is not always banter; some chapters need quiet
  closeness, direct tenderness or plain honesty instead.
- A new term, tool, institution, title, place name or small detail is
  introduced the first time it appears with a natural hook tied to the
  character. If a detail has no hook, give it one or cut it.
- Subtext is used rarely and with a clear trace. If dialogue has two
  layers, at least one character names the hidden one openly.
- Every scene answers early, in a few sentences, what is at stake and what
  the character wants. Detail follows that hook, it does not replace it.
- The reader has not read the world files, only the earlier chapters.
  Every term is introduced in the chapter where it first appears.
